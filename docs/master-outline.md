## Part I — The Thesis: legal education's unfulfilled promise, and the empirical proof

### I.1 The core argument
- **The promise vs. the delivery.** "The Legal Education system fails to live up to its promise to train students to be successful in the practice of law." Practice is changing rapidly; law schools are not keeping up; they do not meet the needs of students, the practice, or society.
- **Three moves** the work makes, repeated verbatim across the reform article and the Method article:
  1. Analyze and *demonstrate* the failure of the current system.
  2. Offer a proven, innovative alternative — the **Legal Practicum Method** — that "incorporates the best of what already works."
  3. Show that modern teaching-and-learning theory lets schools adopt it *without* burning down the institution.
- **The rhetorical spine.** Every section of the flagship article ends with a block titled **"THE PROOF"**, pointing to Sonsteng's peer-reviewed law-review scholarship (see Part VII) — the argument is deliberately built to be *evidence-backed*, not merely hortatory.
- **The historical framing — a "third phase."** Legal education has had two phases: (1) informal **apprenticeship** before law schools; (2) the **Langdellian** case/Socratic model from the late 19th century, essentially unchanged since. The thesis calls for a deliberate **third phase**: a "Legal Education Renaissance."

### I.2 The empirical proof — the skills survey
- **The instrument.** A 53-question survey emailed to **19,077 Minnesota attorneys**; **1,398 responses**; **734** completed the full survey (including the management-skills battery). Part of a longitudinal series — Minnesota studies in **1997–99, 2013, and 2018–19** (with roots in a 1975–2013 series), anchored to Sonsteng's *Minnesota Lawyers Evaluate Law Schools* (26 Wm. Mitchell L. Rev. 327 (2000)).
- **Respondent base (2018/19):** 58% private practice, 16% government, 11% corporate; 72% attended Minnesota law schools; ~49% Twin Cities metro. Cross-checked against national signals (a 2015 survey: **95%** of hiring partners/associates said recent grads lacked key practical skills) and the **MacCrate Report (1992)** and **Carnegie Report (2007)**.
- **What it measured — for each skill, four questions:** (1) importance to practice; (2) how *prepared* the respondent felt at graduation; (3) whether it *could* be learned in law school; (4) the **top-three sources** of the respondent's actual training (from a list of 15 sources: general curriculum, moot court, law-related work, own experience, CLE, simulations, clinics, law review, observing/advice from lawyers & non-lawyers, vendor training, etc.).

#### I.2.a The 17 Legal Practice Skills (surveyed by name)
1. Ability to diagnose and plan solutions for legal problems
2. Ability in legal analysis and reasoning
3. Knowledge of substantive law
4. Knowledge of procedural law
5. Library legal research
6. Computer legal research
7. Factual (fact) gathering
8. Oral communication
9. Written communication
10. Counseling
11. Instilling others' confidence in you
12. Ability to obtain and keep clients
13. Negotiation
14. Understanding and conducting litigation
15. Organization and management of legal work
16. Sensitivity to professional and ethical concerns
17. Drafting legal documents

#### I.2.b The 9 Law Practice Management Skills (surveyed by name)
1. Fee arrangements, pricing, billing
2. Human resources, hiring, support staff
3. Capitalization, investment
4. Project and time management, efficiency
5. Planning, resource allocation, budgeting
6. Marketing, client development
7. Technology, computers, communications
8. Governance, decision-making, long-range strategic planning
9. Interpersonal communications, staff relations

#### I.2.c The findings that indict the status quo
- **All 26 skills are important** to practice; **most respondents believe they can be learned in law school.**
- **The gap.** Law-school curriculum is the single most significant source of training for **only 7 of the 17** legal-practice skills — and for **none of the 9** management skills.
- **The preparedness cliff** (importance vs. self-rated preparedness, illustrative from the tables):
  - *Written communication* — importance **97.4%**, but a leading skill where the curriculum does deliver.
  - *Ability to obtain and keep clients* — importance **80.4%**, preparedness **10.0%** (the starkest gap; law-school curriculum credited by only ~3.9%).
  - *Understanding and conducting litigation* — importance **71.5%**, preparedness **28.7%**.
  - *Counseling*, *Negotiation*, *Organization/management of legal work* — all rated important, all with preparedness in the 30s–40s%.
  - *Library legal research* — importance a low **28.5%** (a skill schools *over*-invest in relative to its perceived practice value).
- **Where lawyers actually learn.** For most practice skills and *every* management skill, the top sources are **"own experience," "law-related work experience," and "observing/advice from other lawyers"** — i.e., the apprenticeship the profession never fully left behind. Schools reclaim primacy only for doctrinal/analytic/research skills (legal reasoning 88.3% from curriculum; library research 93.4%; procedural law 78.1%; ethics 86.8%).
- **The management-skills blackout.** Law-school curriculum is credited in the low single digits as a training source for billing (7.9%), marketing (3.9%), capitalization (2.3%), governance (7.2%), and staff relations (5.0%). A law practice *is a business*; schools teach almost none of it.
- **The conclusion.** Schools "must examine their missions, curriculum, teaching, and learning methods." The survey is the load-bearing evidence for everything that follows.

## Part II — The Legal Practicum Method (the pedagogy)

### II.1 Definition and premise
- **"PRAC·TI·CUM"** — "a course of study designed esp. for the preparation of teachers [read *lawyers*] and clinicians that involves the supervised practical application … of previously studied theory." Learning by *doing*.
- **The core mechanism:** simulate a **small law practice**; students apply legal theory to problem-solving, sharpen written and oral advocacy, and develop the full skill set inside a **simulated law-firm** setting.
- **AALS/ABA cover.** Both associations affirm the importance of practice and management skills; AALS concedes economic constraints block uniform adoption. The Method's claim: *any* committed school can integrate it into the existing system at predictable cost.

### II.2 The four structural pillars ("What is it?")
1. **Course structure** — a consistent format: introductory seminar/briefing by experienced practitioners → analyze the exercise → draft representation agreements → written + oral exercises → written and oral feedback emphasizing improvement. Coverage spans wills, commercial law, civil procedure, criminal law, professional responsibility, torts, family law, employment law.
2. **Instructors & participants** — experienced **lawyers, judges, and business professionals** instruct; **detailed assessment/feedback forms** give consistency + flexibility; feedback is *prompt*. Students work in **pairs (a two-person firm)**.
3. **Writing assignments** — individual and firm; strict **page limits** to force concise, persuasive writing; individual contributions attributed inside firm submissions; each piece assessed by faculty **and** reviewed by writing instructors. Representative annual load (from the article): 8 representation agreements, 8 client billing statements w/ letters, **7 four-page memos**, interrogatories & answers, complaint/answer/counterclaim, dissolution documents, a trial notebook, mediation confidential-info form, motions, an arbitration statement of the case, a will + client letter, and 10-page trial/appellate briefs and memoranda of law.
4. **Exercises** — simulations of a small practice (see Part IV catalog). Number is tunable: fewer = deeper dives; more = the breadth of a real firm's docket.

### II.3 The learning-science foundation
- **Six essential elements of successful education** (the theoretical backbone): **Objectives; Reinforcement & feedback; Positive learning environment; Active classroom; Learning styles; Lesson cycles.**
- **Objectives across three domains** (Bloom-style hierarchies made explicit):
  - **Cognitive** — Knowledge → Comprehension → Application → Analysis → Synthesis → Evaluation.
  - **Affective** — Receiving → Responding → Valuing → Organizing → Forming a personal hierarchy of values.
  - **Psychomotor** — Familiarization → Fundamentals → Development → Adjusting/Adapting → Perfection/Maintenance (courtroom movement, gesture, presence).
- **Feedback timing** as a design principle: too early confuses, too late is "nearly worthless"; the Method engineers *immediate* verbal critique after oral work.
- **Learner-/knowledge-/assessment-centered lens** `[?]` — the scans frame this as the six-elements + three-domains model rather than in the "How People Learn" vocabulary; map the two as: *learner-centered* = learning styles + positive environment + active classroom; *knowledge-centered* = objectives + lesson cycles; *assessment-centered* = reinforcement/feedback + the rubric forms. (Framing is interpretive, consistent with the scans.)
- **Materials architecture** — every exercise packaged in **eight parts**: introduction, objectives, activities, instructions, case file, procedural & factual history, considerations, substantive information.

### II.4 The program and management model
- **Governance:** a full-time **Practicum Director** (owns philosophy, materials, change) + a dedicated **Course Administrator** (scheduling, grading logistics, communications) + part-time lawyer/judge/business instructors.
- **Assessment system:** combines overall performance rankings with exercise-specific scores; predictable annual **budget** approved by the Academic Dean; instructors paid at adjunct hourly rates.

### II.5 "A Simple Plan" — the institutional reform blueprint (NOW / WHY / WHO / WHAT)
- **NOW** — urgent need; past improvements were driven by economic necessity, not strategy; the bar exam predicts practice poorly.
- **WHY** — deficiencies in practical-skills training, outdated Socratic/case pedagogy and timed exams, near-total neglect of management skills, theory–practice gap.
- **WHO** — a coalition (ABA, AALS, deans, boards, faculty, students, alumni, practitioners); institutional self-study; faculty innovation over tenure inertia; leverage existing resources.
- **WHAT — the transformation agenda:** shift away from casebooks/Socratic method; emphasize learning-by-doing; **continuous assessment & feedback** (not one final exam); optimize faculty resources; bar-prep early; **leverage technology and AI**; "assess for success, not rankings."

### II.6 The three-module curriculum (the new degree shape)
- **Module I — Foundational Skills** (one intensive semester): core concepts, the legal system, legal reasoning, research/writing, case briefing, time management, ethics/justice. Gateway to Module II.
- **Module II — Substantive Law & Core Practice Skills** (three semesters): Contracts, Civil Procedure, Torts, Property, Con Law, Criminal Law *paired with* drafting, advanced research, fact development, counseling, negotiation, litigation, practice management, client development, ethics.
- **Module III — Transition to Practice** (two semesters): seminars, workshops, clinics, **practicum simulations**; deep expertise, tangible outputs, teamwork/leadership, repeated practice + feedback.

### II.7 The Practicum Method Teaching System (the three-step cycle inside every course)
- **FSS-I — Faculty Supervised Study I:** independent pre-work (texts, casebooks, online, prepared materials); competence checked via short papers/essays/MCQs/oral exercise.
- **IRP — Intensive Residential Practicum:** the core; face-to-face, small-group, faculty-guided, real-world simulation.
- **FSS-II — Faculty Supervised Study II:** comprehensive applied assessment integrating FSS-I + IRP (memos, problem discussion).

### II.8 The faculty and cost argument (why it's affordable)
- **Restructure, don't expand.** Gradually convert a share of tenure-track lines to **long-term contract + adjunct** teaching faculty on a **step-and-grade ladder** (Assistant Lecturer → … → Master Teacher), with salaries pegged to an external benchmark (e.g., assistant county-attorney pay).
- **The cost model (from the Practicum Series doc):** a school paying **40 full-time faculty at $139,026/yr = $5,562,480**. A **50% reduction of tenure-track** frees enough to hire ~**two long-term contract teachers per tenure-track line replaced**, **tripling teaching time at the same or lower cost.**
- **Illustrative administrative structure:** Dean + Associate Deans; ~**17 tenure-track** faculty (research, educational R&D, material development, advanced lectures) alongside ~**40 full-time long-term contract** faculty delivering Modules 1–3 across Lectures / Faculty Supervised Studies / Intensive Residential Practicum. Dual governance keeps research *and* teaching valued.

## Part III — The Skills Practicum course (the pedagogy, running live)

*The operational proof-of-concept: **General Practice: Skills Practicum**, Mitchell Hamline (Prof. Sonsteng, Director; Jen Miller, Administrator; Judge Kellie Charles co-director in 2023). HyFlex, delivered on Zoom.*

### III.1 The working-law-firm simulation
- **Two-person firms** for the whole semester; firm named for both partners alphabetically; own letterhead, address, case-file number.
- Work must be **realistically balanced** between partners and **not** split by exercise. Firms serve opposing counsel, file to the "court," and manage their own docket.
- **Individual vs. firm work:** individual = first assignment, weekly time sheets, four-page memos, Learning Portfolio, Reflective Report; firm = everything else (agreements, briefs, pleadings, billing).

### III.2 Credit tiers and hours
- **3 credits** — 5 exercises (Tort, Professional Responsibility, Non-Compete, Real Estate, Arbitration), **141 hours**, *no* DWI, does **not** satisfy the advanced-writing/long-paper requirement.
- **5 credits** — all **6 exercises** (adds **DWI**), **235 hours**, satisfies the long-paper/Advanced Research & Writing requirement (requires the two legal-writing meetings).

### III.3 Weekly rhythm (Fall 2023 arc, illustrative)
- **Aug (weeks 1–3):** read case files; briefings for PR, Arbitration, Real Estate, Tort, DWI, Non-Compete; produce **preliminary case-analysis four-page memos** and representation agreements; Time Sheets begin.
- **Sept:** Arbitration statements of the case + joint statement; arbitrations (trial garb); Real Estate SSNP + three-way negotiation; PR Director's/Respondent's briefs; DWI memo/agreement/SSNP + negotiation & hearing before Judge Olmstead.
- **Oct:** Non-Compete plaintiff/defendant motion papers + oral-argument & deposition **workshops**; PR oral arguments before OLPR panel; DWI court trials; final billings; **Learning Portfolio** due.
- **Nov:** Tort pleadings/discovery/motions in limine/jury instructions/special verdict; opening & direct/cross **workshops**; Tort **pretrial conferences**; **full-day final jury trials** (8:00 a.m.–2:30 p.m., volunteer jurors on Zoom).
- **Dec:** final billing; last Time Sheet; **Reflective Report**; course survey.

### III.4 Student deliverables (the artifact set)
- **Preliminary case-analysis four-page memos** (7 across the course) — a fixed analytic template: governing law; strengths/weaknesses of both sides; issues; suggested solutions/outcomes; theory and themes; elements to prevail; liabilities/remedies. Must use **Minnesota law** (cited cases are reference only; cited statutes are controlling). Double-spaced, 1-inch margins, 12-pt.
- **Representation agreements** with client cover letters (consider contingency/flat/hourly/capped fees).
- **Correspondence & cover letters** to client, opposing counsel, clerk of court; **proof of service** on opposing counsel (ex parte contact is unethical and barred).
- **Pleadings & motion practice** — summons/complaint/answer/counterclaim/reply; interrogatories + requests for production; motions in limine, injunction motions + supporting/opposing memoranda; jury instructions, special verdict forms, proposed orders; affidavits of service; civil cover sheets; venue (Ramsey County) mechanics.
- **ADR documents** — arbitration statement of the case + joint statement; strategic settlement & negotiation plans (SSNPs); joint summary memos; joint purchase agreement.
- **Weekly time sheets** (13 total) feeding end-of-exercise **client billing statements** — firm name/contact, client info, dates, description drawn from time sheets, hourly rate + subtotals, expenses, fees received, balance due, due date.
- **Learning Portfolio (3–5 pp.)** and **Reflective Report (3–5 pp.)** — reflective learning grounded in **Kolb's experiential-learning cycle** (Experience → Reflective observation → Conceptualization → Active experimentation), via John R. McLachlan's LLM course notes. Reflection is treated as the missing ingredient of practice ("doing is not enough").

### III.5 Grading, rubrics, and the feedback engine
- **Assessment & feedback forms** (rubrics) exist **per exercise and per oral event**, available online; students are told to review the relevant rubric *as preparation*.
- **Weighted, points-based grade** (Assessment Chart totals **~1,438 points**), e.g.:
  - *Tort* exercise **325 pts** (four-page memo 42; pleadings 21; interrogatories 28; joint statement 28; pretrial 56; **final trial 150**).
  - *DWI* **316.5 pts** (negotiation 101.5; settlement conf. 56; court trial letter-graded A=75/A-=71/B+=67…).
  - *Arbitration* **202 pts** (arbitration letter-graded A=132/A-=126…; statement of the case 28).
  - *Professional Responsibility* **207**; *Non-Compete* **189**; *Real Estate* **185.5**; **13 time sheets = 13 pts**.
- **Consistency, improvement, attendance, and timeliness** all feed the final grade (late work −1 pt/24 hrs).
- **Re-write rule (the "unlimited-reps" seed):** any assignment scored **below 6** on a criterion may be re-written within **one week**; re-writes capped at 6; must be submitted as a redlined/"Compare"-tool PDF with the original rubric attached — an explicit *revise-against-the-rubric* loop.
- **Two mandatory legal-writing meetings** (~20 min each) with a dedicated writing instructor; end-of-term body-of-work rating: excellent / good / satisfactory (not scored into the grade, but improvement is noted; remediation required if unsatisfactory).
- **Teaching teams** per exercise: a lawyer-teacher (briefing), a written grader / grading team, and an oral judge/lawyer team (e.g., PR oral panels staffed by the **Office of Lawyers Professional Responsibility**; a large adjunct-faculty roster of practicing lawyers and sitting judges).

## Part IV — The Exercise Catalog (the simulations and case files)

*Each exercise = a full matter with a complete record, mapped to the survey's skills. Trial garb ("courtroom attire") is required for all oral events.*

### IV.1 The six General-Practice matters (case files)
1. **Arbitration — *Midstate University (Employer) v. Pat Rogers (Employee) & State Professional Employee Union (SPEU)*.** Employment/labor arbitration. Each side one witness (employer: **Officer M.J. Troy**; employee: **Pat Rogers**). 120-min hearing: openings, direct/cross, final arguments, 20-min critique. *Skills: legal analysis, drafting (statement of the case), fact development, direct/cross, oral advocacy, negotiation of a joint statement.*
2. **Professional Responsibility — *In the Matter of the Petition for Disciplinary Action Against Timothy B. Halbrock, Attorney at Law*.** Attorney discipline/disbarment. Director's brief + Respondent's brief (10-pp., Minn. R. Civ. App. P. 132/130.02); oral argument before an assigned judge + **two OLPR panelists**; issues split between partners, 15 min each, rebuttal reserved for the Director. *Skills: appellate brief-writing, standard of review, ethics, oral argument under questioning.*
3. **Tort / Personal Injury — *Reagan v. Jacobson*.** Auto negligence, the capstone **full-day jury trial**. Full pleadings + discovery + motions in limine + jury instructions + special verdict; **the diagram** is the load-bearing stipulated exhibit; insurance excluded; each partner must do an opening *or* closing plus ≥1 direct and ≥1 cross; volunteer jurors deliberate on Zoom. *Skills: the entire litigation arc, evidence, trial advocacy, teamwork.*
4. **Real Estate — *Peters, Taylor and Thomas*.** Three-way transactional negotiation (buyer, seller, and **Dr. Gregory Thomas**' counsel, role-played by adjunct Creig Andreasen). Turns on **easement / revocable license / curb** and city power over roads. SSNP + joint purchase agreement + joint summary memo; the *only* exercise with **confidential facts** distributed per side. *Skills: transactional drafting, multi-party negotiation, counseling, creative problem-solving.*
5. **Criminal / DWI — *State v. James*** (defendant **Alex James**; witnesses **Shannon Draper**, **Officer J.J. Liftin**). 5-credit only. 15-min plea **negotiation** → 45-min **hearing** before **Judge Olmstead** → 2-hr **court trial** (no intoxilyzer/alcohol-influence-chart references). *Skills: criminal procedure, plea bargaining, motion practice, bench trial advocacy.*
6. **Non-Compete / Employment — *SSHC v. Baines*.** Motion for temporary injunction: plaintiff & defendant memoranda (10-pp.), exhibits, proposed orders; 90-min **oral argument + settlement conference** (creative settlement encouraged). *Skills: injunction standards, persuasive motion writing, oral advocacy, settlement.*

### IV.2 Additional matters described in the Method article (the extensible bank)
- **Commercial Law / UCC** — sale of a **dental X-ray machine**, breach of warranties, arbitration & mediation.
- **Criminal / Juvenile Delinquency** — juvenile **shoplifting** representation, negotiation, court appearance.
- **Family Law / Dissolution of Marriage** — maintenance, custody, property division, name change (dissolution prehearing statement, joint disposition conference report, summary of position).
- **Will Drafting / Estate Planning** — basic spousal wills from detailed client info + explanatory client letter.

### IV.3 The Practicum *Series* architecture (the reusable exercise engine)
- **Modalities the series builds toward:** court & jury trials, **depositions, arbitrations, mediations**, motions/oral arguments, **negotiations**, research, client advising.
- **Every exercise file contains:** Procedural & Factual History; complete Facts; Party & Expert **Witness Reports/Statements/Depositions**; Applicable Law; Pleadings & Agendas; a preliminary **Case & Strategic Analysis**.
- **Learning Materials per exercise:** a **Planning Guide & Check List** (witness prep, substance, presentation) and **Learning Objectives** split into **Oral** and **Written** — each an explicit measurable outcome.
- **Doctrinal series modules** (each with its own topic map): **Advocacy** (motions, trial prep, opening, direct, cross, evidence, exhibits, closing), **Torts** (intentional torts → negligence → strict liability → damages/defamation/privacy), **Civil Procedure** (jurisdiction, preclusion, complex litigation, choice of law).
- **The per-topic lesson cycle:** Intro/educational model & objectives → assignments to main texts → lecture → PowerPoint + readings → other experts' perspectives → primary reference (Rules of Evidence/Procedure) → secondary reference (texts/treatises) → demonstrations → wrap-up video → **questions from judges or jury** → **Options: Test · Practice · Homework · Feedback · Repeat.** *(This "Repeat" loop is the analog root of the Centaur layer's unlimited reps — Part VI.)*

## Part V — The Commons: *Teaching & Learning Law & Business* and the open-resource platform

### V.1 The Open Resource Tool (2020, Center for Law & Business, Mitchell Hamline)
- **Authorship:** Prof. John Sonsteng (Director, Center for Law & Business) with **Willow Anderson** and **Michael Carlson** (Minnesota State Bar), student contributors, and a long roster of faculty/practitioner contributors — including **Roger Haydock** (co-author of *Trialbook*; see V.4) and **Jennifer Miller**.
- **The open-source analogy (the platform thesis):** just as freely available source code lets independent teams collaborate and improve software, an **"Open Resource Tool"** makes legal-education materials freely available so the whole field can iterate. Legal education has been "comparatively insular"; the fix is a Wikipedia-style brain trust.
- **Structure:** a **library of modules** (articles, research, syllabi, **practicum exercises**, innovations) + a **discussion forum**; publishing categories from "Imagining an Uncertain Future" to law-school courses, bar-association programs, international efforts, and centers/institutes. Manuscript norms: 800–3,000 words, Bluebook endnotes, authors retain copyright.
- **The business-of-law argument (companion to Part I):** a law practice *is* a business; the same Minnesota studies show schools teach almost none of marketing, entrepreneurship, finance, technology, or management. Reviews national peers (Georgetown Business Law Scholars, Northwestern's entrepreneurship center, Quinnipiac's Business Concepts Bootcamp, Yale/Columbia clinics, etc.) and Richard Susskind's futurism (AI, commoditization, ADR displacing courts).

### V.2 The Center for Law & Business (C-LAB) curriculum
- **Law & Business Certificate:** 12 required credits (Business Organizations; **Lawyer as Business Owner**; Transactions & Settlements; Commercial Law Survey *or* Secured Transactions) + 9 elective credits + a **4-credit practical-experience** requirement (clinic/externship/residency/**Business Practicum** or **General Practice: Skills Practicum**) + a business-topic long paper + 3.0 GPA. The Skills Practicum is thus a *certified* practical-experience pathway inside C-LAB.

## Part VI — The Centaur layer: AI first-pass + human coaching, mapped to every component

*The synthesis move: the scans already contain the two halves of a human+AI ("centaur") pedagogy — (a) explicit **rubrics** for every deliverable, and (b) a **revise-and-repeat** loop (re-writes capped at 6; "Test · Practice · Homework · Feedback · **Repeat**"). The bottleneck was always human grader time. AI removes it for the **first pass**, reserving scarce faculty time for high-value coaching. This section is the proposed layer; grounded in the scans' rubrics/repeat loop + the article's explicit call to "leverage technology and AI."*

### VI.1 The pairing principle
- **AI = first-pass rubric critique + unlimited reps.** Every exercise already ships a machine-readable rubric (Part III.5). AI grades a draft against that rubric instantly and lets the student iterate **without a one-week / cap-at-6 limit** — the human constraint that shaped the original re-write rule dissolves.
- **Human faculty = judgment, presence, and the oral crucible.** Lawyers/judges keep the briefings, the live oral events (arbitration, argument, negotiation, trial), and the affective/psychomotor coaching AI can't stage.

### VI.2 Component-by-component mapping
- **Four-page memos** → AI checks the 7-point analytic template (governing law, strengths/weaknesses, issues, solutions, theory/themes, elements, liabilities/remedies) and Minnesota-law usage; faculty coach judgment and strategy.
- **Briefs & motions** → AI checks structure, rule compliance (Minn. R. Civ. App. P. 132), citation, page limits; faculty coach persuasion and standard-of-review argument.
- **Representation agreements & billing** → AI validates fee logic, required billing elements, time-sheet-to-invoice consistency; faculty coach client counseling and business judgment (the management skills schools *don't* teach).
- **Negotiations / SSNPs** → AI stress-tests the plan (interests, BATNA, confidentiality, creative options) via unlimited role-play reps; faculty/adjunct role-players run the graded live negotiation.
- **Oral advocacy (arbitration, PR argument, DWI trial, tort trial)** → AI runs unlimited rehearsal with judge/jury-style questioning + immediate critique; human judges preside over the graded event and deliver the affective/psychomotor coaching.
- **Learning Portfolio / Reflective Report** → AI prompts deeper Kolb-cycle reflection; the Director reads for insight and the exceptional cases.

### VI.3 Why this is faithful, not bolted-on
- It preserves the Method's non-negotiables — **clear objectives, immediate feedback, active learning, repeated practice** — and simply makes feedback **instant and infinite**. It also finally makes the **cost model** trivially true: AI-assisted first-pass grading pushes teaching capacity well past the "triple the teaching time at the same cost" target of Part II.8. `[?]` Live product bindings (e.g., MootLoop-style simulators, *Trialbook* AI companions) come from the project's other workstreams, not these scans.

## Part VII — Proposed Magnum Opus structure (consolidated Book) — and its sources

*A single book that layers the Argument, the Method, the Course, the Commons, and the Platform. Each part maps to existing Sonsteng articles/materials so the Magnum Opus is an assembly of proven, peer-reviewed pieces, not a new draft from zero.*

### Front matter
- Title + the "third phase / Legal Education Renaissance" framing; dedication to the practitioner-teachers; the recurring **"THE PROOF"** device carried through every part.

### Cross-cutting through-lines (the spine of the whole work)
1. **Evidence first** — every prescription is anchored to the survey ("THE PROOF").
2. **Learn by doing, then reflect** — simulation + Kolb reflection, never one without the other.
3. **Rubric + repeat** — explicit objectives, immediate feedback, unlimited iteration (the seam where AI plugs in).
4. **Affordable by restructuring, not spending** — contract/adjunct faculty + AI first-pass, triple the teaching time at equal cost.
5. **Open and collaborative** — an open-resource Commons, not a walled garden; the profession improves the materials like open-source code.
