## 1. The 17 Legal Practice Skills

Order and naming follow Sonsteng's "Research" narrative list (the numbered survey tables use
lightly re-worded labels; noted where they differ).

1. Ability to diagnose and plan solutions for legal problems
2. Ability in legal analysis and reasoning
3. Knowledge of substantive law
4. Knowledge of procedural law
5. Library legal research *(narrative: "Skills to conduct legal library research")*
6. Computer legal research *(narrative: "Knowledge of computer legal research")*
7. Fact gathering
8. Oral communication
9. Written communication
10. Counseling
11. Instilling others' confidence in you *(narrative: "The ability to instill others' confidence in their work")*
12. Ability to obtain and keep clients
13. Negotiation
14. Litigation *(tables: "Understanding and conducting litigation")*
15. Organization and management of legal work
16. Sensitivity to professional and ethical concerns
17. Drafting legal documents *(narrative: "Ability to draft legal documents")*

Count = 17, matching Sonsteng's own statement that "Law school curriculum is the most
significant source of training for 7 of the **17** Legal Practice Skills."

## Findings

### Preliminary findings (as Sonsteng states them)
- All 17 Legal Practice Skills are important to the practice of law.
- All 9 Law Practice Management Skills are important to the practice of law.
- Most respondents believe these skills could be successfully learned in law school.
- Law school curriculum is the most significant source of training for **7 of the 17** Legal Practice Skills.
- Law school curriculum was **not** a significant source of training for **any** of the 9 Law Practice Management Skills.

### Importance rankings — Legal Practice Skills (Table 1, % rating skill important)
Highest-ranked:
- Written communication — **97.4%** (highest)
- Ability in legal analysis and legal reasoning — 96.8%
- Ability to diagnose and plan for legal problems — 96.1%
- Oral communication — 96.0%
- Drafting legal documents — 91.0%

Lowest-ranked:
- Understanding and conducting litigation — 71.5%
- Library legal research — **28.5%** (far and away the lowest)

### Preparedness rankings — Legal Practice Skills (Table 2, % feeling prepared by law school)
Best-prepared:
- Ability in legal analysis and legal reasoning — 84.3%
- Computer legal research — 81.7%
- Written communication — 78.6%
- Oral communication — 72.7%

Worst-prepared:
- Understanding and conducting litigation — 28.7%
- **Ability to obtain and keep clients — 10.0%** (lowest of all)

### The importance-vs-preparedness gap (the paper's central point)
Computed as Importance (Table 1) − Preparedness (Table 2). Largest gaps — skills rated highly
important but for which graduates feel least prepared:

| Legal Practice Skill | Importance | Preparedness | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ability to obtain and keep clients** | 80.4 | 10.0 | **70.4** (largest) |
| Organization and management of legal work | 85.2 | 30.7 | 54.5 |
| Counseling | 86.4 | 39.1 | 47.3 |
| Instilling others' confidence in you | 89.0 | 42.0 | 47.0 |
| Drafting legal documents | 91.0 | 45.0 | 46.0 |
| Understanding and conducting litigation | 71.5 | 28.7 | 42.8 |
| Negotiation | 86.2 | 43.6 | 42.6 |

By contrast, the smallest gaps (skills law school prepares well relative to importance) are legal
analysis/reasoning (96.8 → 84.3) and computer legal research (87.1 → 81.7).

### Law Practice Management Skills — preparedness (Table 5, % feeling prepared)
Preparedness is uniformly low; law school prepares graduates for essentially none of these:
- Technology, computers, communications — 53.4% (highest)
- Project and time management, efficiency — 40.3%
- Interpersonal communications, staff relations — 39.6%
- Planning, resource allocation, budgeting — 13.7%
- Governance, decision-making, long-range strategic planning — 13.4%
- Marketing, client development — 10.2%
- Fee arrangements, pricing, billing — 9.0%
- Human resources, hiring, support staff — 8.9%
- Capitalization, investment — **4.0%** (lowest)

Table 4 (importance of Law Practice Management skills) ranks Project & time management highest and
Capitalization/investment lowest, but the percentage column is badly garbled in the scan (OCR
returned run-together digits such as "966662"), so exact importance percentages for the management
skills are not reliably recoverable from these two scans.

### Sources of training (Tables 7–8)
- Where law school *does* dominate as the top training source: legal analysis/reasoning (88.3%),
  library legal research (93.4%), computer legal research (84.6%), substantive law (84.7%),
  written communication (66.0%), procedural law (78.1%), and sensitivity to professional/ethical
  concerns (86.8%) — i.e., the "7 of 17" skills.
- Client-facing and management skills are learned overwhelmingly from "own experience,"
  "observing other lawyers," and "advice from other lawyers." Law school's share is near-zero for
  obtaining/keeping clients (3.9%), instilling confidence (7.8%), fee arrangements (7.9%),
  capitalization (2.3%), marketing (3.9%), and interpersonal/staff relations (5.0%).

## Provenance / confidence

- **Both numbered lists (17 + 9): high confidence.** They appear verbatim in *both* target scans,
  and each list's count is independently confirmed by Sonsteng's prose ("7 of the 17…", "any of the
  9…").
- **Skill naming:** The 17-skill list has two slightly different phrasings across the paper — a
  narrative "Research" list and the table labels. Both phrasings are shown above; no skill is
  ambiguous or invented. In particular "Litigation" and "Understanding and conducting litigation"
  are the same skill; "Instilling others' confidence in you" and "…in their work" are the same skill.
- **OCR corrections applied:** none were needed inside the two lists themselves (the target-file
  passages were clean). Elsewhere in the scans the OCR mangles "Firm"→"Finn", "Forms"→"Fonns",
  "information"→"infonnation", etc.; those do not affect the skill names.
- **Table 1–3, 5, 7, 8 percentages: high confidence** (clean in the scan).
- **Table 4 (importance of management skills) percentages: low confidence** — digit column is
  run-together in OCR and not reliably separable. Rank order (Project/time mgmt highest →
  Capitalization lowest) is recoverable; exact percentages are not. Marked accordingly above.
