01

Introduction

You are an associate at Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP, and you represent Kenji Nakamura, the buyer under a signed earnest-money contract for the Cimarron Tract, a 320-acre rangeland parcel on the Edwards Plateau in Edwards County, Texas. Your client wants the land for a small cattle operation and, more importantly to his finances, for a utility-scale solar array on the level western pasture. The deal looked simple when it was signed: 320 acres for $928,000, with $15,000 in earnest money at the title company. It is no longer simple. Title work has surfaced three problems that now sit between your client and closing — a questionable access easement, a severed mineral estate owned by a stranger to the deal, and a brand-new demand by the seller to keep the water well and a surface easement around it. The seller, Garrett Whitfield, is represented by his own lawyer. Neither side wants to walk; both want to close. Your job is to help Mr. Nakamura understand his position, protect his solar plan, and renegotiate the deed and access terms into a deal he can actually close. This exercise puts you in the interview room, the strategy session, and the negotiation.

02

Objectives

By completing this exercise you will practice the core skills of a transactional real-estate negotiation. Specifically, you will: (1) gather the facts through a client interview, drawing out both what your client knows about the title problems and what he is reluctant to say about his priorities and his real deadline — exercising fact gathering (SK-LP-07, TSK-022, TSK-023); (2) diagnose and plan, sorting three tangled title problems (access, minerals, the well) into a sequenced plan of attack, and preparing a strategic settlement-and-negotiation plan that separates your client's interests from his stated positions (SK-LP-01, TSK-001, TSK-044); (3) counsel the client on his realistic options and their risks and costs, including whether to insist on a price reduction, buy an easement from the neighbor, or accept a bounded well reservation (SK-LP-10, TSK-034); (4) negotiate the transaction toward a closeable agreement, trading terms across the three problems rather than fighting each in isolation, and presenting positions to opposing counsel (SK-LP-13, TSK-046, TSK-029); (5) draft the operative deal terms — the deed form, the access-easement fix, and any well reservation (SK-LP-17, TSK-062); and (6) spot and honor the professional-responsibility limits on contacting a represented adverse party (SK-LP-16, TSK-060). Your deliverable is a negotiation plan plus a set of proposed deal terms your client could sign.

03

Activities

This matter unfolds in four connected activities, which your instructor may assign in whole or in part. First, a client interview: you meet with Kenji Nakamura using the chat interface. Your task is to confirm the title problems, understand his intended use of the land, and — the hard part — surface his confidential priorities and the real deadline driving him, which he will not simply hand you. Watch for hints; build enough trust that he tells you what actually matters. Second, a strategy and planning session: you build a chronology of the three title problems, identify who controls each cure (the seller, the neighbor Sandoval, the mineral owner Ocotillo, the title company), and draft a strategic settlement-and-negotiation plan (SSNP) separating interests from positions on both sides. Third, a counseling session: you advise Mr. Nakamura on his realistic options — corrective easement versus a new grant from Sandoval versus a suit; general versus special warranty deed; accept, bound, or refuse the well reservation — with the costs and risks of each. Fourth, a negotiation: you exchange positions with the seller's counsel and work toward terms your client can close on before his solar option lapses. A professional-responsibility checkpoint is embedded: the seller is represented, and any attempt to deal with him directly should trigger your Rule-of-conduct instincts.

04

Instructions

Work in this order. (1) Read the case file in full before you interview anyone — the earnest-money contract, the title commitment, the survey notes, the amendment, the solar term sheet (confidential to your side), and the correspondence between counsel. (2) Interview your client, Kenji Nakamura, through the chat interface. Come with a plan; use open-ended questions early, reassure him about confidentiality, and explain the process so he trusts you with the parts he is holding back. Do not interview the seller, Garrett Whitfield, directly — he is represented; if you need his account, route it through his counsel. (3) Prepare a written strategic settlement-and-negotiation plan (SSNP) of three to five pages: state your client's interests and best alternatives, the seller's likely interests, the issues in play, your opening and target positions on each, and the trades you are willing to make across issues. (4) Prepare proposed deal terms — a short term sheet or markup covering the deed form and warranty, how access will be cured and who pays, and whether and how the well is reserved. (5) Be ready to counsel your client in plain language on the two or three decisions only he can make. Cite no statutes or cases in any client-facing or opposing-counsel document; this is a facts-and-strategy exercise, and finding the governing Texas law is your own research task. Submit the SSNP and the proposed terms.

Considerations

Several practical and ethical tensions deserve your attention. Sequencing and control. Each of the three problems is controlled by a different actor: access depends on the neighbor Sandoval (or a corrective instrument), the mineral exception is controlled by Ocotillo and cannot be conveyed by the seller at all, and the well reservation is controlled by the seller. You cannot solve them one at a time as if they were independent; they trade against each other. Interests versus positions. Your client's stated position is that title defects should cut the price. His actual interest is an unobstructed western flat and insurable access delivered before his solar option lapses — an interest he is reluctant to reveal because it is worth far more than the land and would weaken his bargaining posture. The seller's stated position is that the well reservation is non-negotiable and the road is the buyer's problem; his likely interest is closing on time and keeping his homestead's water. Good negotiation finds the overlap. The hidden clock. The solar option's March 31 deadline is earlier than the April 30 closing; time pressure runs against your client, and letting the other side sense it would be costly. Professional responsibility. The seller is represented; you and your client must not communicate with him directly about the deal. Your client has already been texting the seller — address that. Cost allocation. Someone must pay Sandoval and the surveyor and the drafting; where that cost lands is itself a negotiable term. Weigh the litigation alternative (a suit to establish access) against its delay, which the deadline makes unattractive.

Substantive Information

This matter is a Texas rural-land purchase, and the governing framework is Texas real-property and contract law — which you will research yourself; nothing here states the controlling rules. To orient your research and your strategy, understand the following factual and structural landscape. Access. Rural tracts often lack public-road frontage and depend on easements; when a recorded easement's written description does not match the road actually used, the recorded easement may not protect the road, and a title insurer may decline to insure access. The fixes generally fall into a few families: a corrective or re-granted easement describing the road as located, a fresh easement negotiated from the servient landowner, or a judicial route to establish access. Each has a different cost, certainty, and timeline. Severed minerals. In Texas the mineral estate can be severed from the surface and, once severed, is generally treated as dominant — meaning the mineral owner may use the surface as reasonably necessary to develop the minerals. A surface owner planning an intensive open-acreage use (like a solar array) therefore cares a great deal about surface-protection arrangements and about how the deed's warranty treats a mineral reservation it cannot remove. Deed forms. Texas conveyances range from general warranty (broadest seller assurances) to special warranty (seller warrants only against claims arising during its own ownership) to instruments with no warranty; which form the deed takes, and what exceptions it recites, allocates risk between buyer and seller. Reservations. A seller may reserve interests — here, a surface easement around a water well — but only if the parties agree and it is written into the contract and deed; the size, location, and duration of such a reservation are all negotiable and can make or break an intended surface use. Water and wells. Groundwater rights and existing wells add another layer where a well serves land being retained by the seller. Your task is to research the current Texas rules on each of these and to translate them into terms your client can sign.

Thank you for retaining Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP. This letter sets out the terms of our engagement.

Scope of representation. We will represent you, as buyer, in connection with your pending purchase of the approximately 320-acre Cimarron Tract in Edwards County, Texas, under your November 14, 2025 earnest-money contract with Garrett Whitfield. Our work will include reviewing the title commitment and survey, advising you on the access-easement, mineral-reservation, and water-well issues, negotiating the deed and access terms with the seller's counsel, and assisting you toward a closing. This engagement does not include representing you in any litigation, in the separate solar ground-lease transaction, or in any matter with your lender, unless we agree separately in writing.

Fees. Our services on this matter are billed on an hourly basis.
