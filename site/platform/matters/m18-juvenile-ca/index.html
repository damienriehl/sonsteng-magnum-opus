01

Introduction

You are an attorney at Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP, and you have been retained to represent a sixteen-year-old, Jasmine Cordero, in a juvenile matter in the Juvenile Division of the Superior Court of California, County of Alta Mesa. The People allege that Jasmine took part in vandalizing school property and several cars in the Encinal Ridge High School parking lot after a Friday-night basketball game — spray paint on the gym wall and on two vehicles, and a keyed scratch down the side of a teacher's car. Jasmine tells a very different story: she was there with two friends, Kayla and Andre, but she never sprayed or scratched anything — she was filming the whole thing on her phone. She also believes the school pulled her in first, ahead of the other two, because of a dress-code complaint she had filed months earlier against the assistant principal who identified her. The People have offered an informal diversion program that would leave Jasmine with no adjudication and no wardship if she completes it — but diversion ordinarily asks the minor to accept responsibility, and Jasmine says she did nothing wrong. Your client is frightened and her single mother is overwhelmed and stretched thin. Your job in this exercise is to interview the family, develop the facts, and help them choose between accepting diversion and contesting the petition — all while handling a vulnerable minor client and a live professional-responsibility problem.

02

Objectives

By completing this exercise you will practice the core competencies of a juvenile-defense intake and disposition workup. Specifically, you will: (1) Gather facts from a frightened minor through a developmentally appropriate, non-leading interview, drawing out the client's own account before the adults around her reshape it — exercising fact gathering (SK-LP-07), TSK-004 (diagnose the client's legal problem at intake), TSK-022 (conduct a client intake interview), and TSK-023 (investigate and develop the facts). (2) Counsel a distressed, vulnerable client and an anxious parent without losing the child's confidence or the parent's trust — exercising counseling (SK-LP-10), TSK-037 (counsel a distressed or vulnerable client), and TSK-034 (advise the client on options and risks). (3) Diagnose the matter and build a disposition strategy, weighing the informal-diversion offer against a contested jurisdictional hearing and preparing a negotiation plan on terms and restitution — exercising diagnosis and planning (SK-LP-01), negotiation (SK-LP-13), TSK-025 (build and maintain a chronology of facts), TSK-035 (counsel on settlement versus proceeding), TSK-044 (prepare a strategic settlement and negotiation plan), and TSK-045 (conduct a settlement or plea negotiation). (4) Spot and navigate the professional-responsibility issues — most pointedly the Rule 4.2 no-contact problem posed by the represented co-participant, plus the confidentiality questions raised by a parent's presence and by the un-counseled school questioning — exercising sensitivity to professional and ethical concerns (SK-LP-16), TSK-059 (safeguard client confidences and privilege), and TSK-060 (comply with the rules of professional conduct).

03

Activities

This exercise unfolds as a sequence of connected activities. First, you will review the case file — the sheriff's SRO incident report, the wardship petition, the diversion offer, the surveillance and phone-video still-frame log, the property-damage estimate, the prior dress-code referral, the group-chat excerpt, and the witness statements — and build a working chronology, flagging the evidentiary gaps (the dim, unresolved footage; the missing phone video; the incomplete text thread). Second, you will conduct a simulated client interview with Jasmine through the chat engine, practicing open-ended, non-judgmental questioning to draw out her account, including the facts she will share only once she trusts you. Third, you will interview her mother, Ana, managing her distress and guilt while gathering what she knows. Fourth, you will consider whether and how you may contact the co-participant, Kayla — and confront the Rule 4.2 barrier when you try. Fifth, you will analyze the diversion offer against the risks of a contested jurisdictional hearing and prepare a short strategic settlement-and-negotiation plan, including your restitution position given that Jasmine's own alleged role is disputed and the keyed car appears to be someone else's doing. Finally, you will counsel the family on the decision, in language a scared sixteen-year-old and an overwhelmed parent can actually use.

04

Instructions

Work through the exercise in the order the activities describe, and produce the following deliverables. (1) A fact chronology of the incident and the procedural posture, expressly identifying every place the accounts conflict and every gap in the proof. (2) An interview plan for Jasmine and a separate one for Ana, listing your opening, your open-ended prompts, the rapport-building moves you will use, and the sensitive areas you expect to reach only with trust; then conduct both interviews in the chat engine. (3) A short professional-responsibility memo identifying the Rule 4.2 issue with the represented co-participant and stating exactly what you may and may not do, plus a note on how the un-counseled school questioning and the parent's presence affect confidentiality and any statements. (4) A strategic settlement-and-negotiation plan (SSNP) comparing acceptance of diversion with a contested jurisdictional hearing, setting your position on responsibility-acknowledgment and restitution, and identifying your best- and worst-case outcomes and your client's priorities. (5) A counseling script or outline for advising Jasmine and Ana on the decision, written for a lay audience. Do your own legal research for any doctrine you rely on; the packet is facts-only by design. Keep the child's stated priorities — avoiding a record, not being the one who names her friends, and being believed that she didn't do it — at the center of your recommendation.

Procedural & Factual History

On the evening of Friday, April 10, 2026, after a home basketball game, an incident occurred in the parking lot at Encinal Ridge High School in Encinal Springs, County of Alta Mesa. Two teenagers spray-painted the gymnasium wall and two vehicles, and a third vehicle — a teacher's sedan — was also keyed down the driver's side. Sixteen-year-old Jasmine Cordero was in the lot with the two friends, Kayla Trejo and Andre Sotelo; Jasmine says she was filming on her phone and never applied paint or scratched anything. The damage was discovered Saturday morning, April 11. On Monday, April 13, Assistant Principal Gerald Corvino reviewed dim security footage, said he recognized Jasmine, and pulled her from class first; he and the School Resource Officer, Deputy Omar Haddad, questioned her in his office before her mother arrived. Deputy Haddad completed an incident report dated April 14 and referred the matter to Probation and the District Attorney's juvenile unit. On May 4, 2026, the District Attorney filed a wardship petition captioned In re J.C., a Minor, alleging conduct that would be vandalism if committed by an adult, assigned to the Juvenile Division. Jasmine appeared at her first hearing on May 12, 2026, and the allegations were denied. On May 26, 2026, the People offered an informal diversion resolution — community service, an accountability class, and restitution — that would end in dismissal with no adjudication if completed, with a decision deadline of June 19, 2026. Kayla Trejo has been charged separately and is represented by the county public defender; Andre Sotelo has also been charged separately.

07

Considerations

Several tensions make this matter harder than it looks. Intent and participation are contested. Whether Jasmine is responsible turns on whether merely being present and filming makes her a participant, and the physical evidence is genuinely ambiguous: the security footage is dim and does not resolve who did what, and the one neutral eyewitness says Jasmine held up her phone and never touched the wall or the cars — while placing the keying on Andre. The diversion decision is not obvious. Diversion offers a clean outcome with no record — exactly what Jasmine says she wants — but it typically requires accepting responsibility, and Jasmine maintains she did nothing wrong. Contesting the petition preserves her innocence claim and exploits the proof gaps, but a contested jurisdictional hearing risks an adjudication and the very record she fears. The client is a child, and the adults around her can distort the facts. A frightened minor may agree with whatever an authority figure suggests; a guilt-ridden, exhausted parent may push for the fastest resolution or, conversely, may want to fight. You must protect the child's voice and her stated priorities. There is a live Rule 4.2 trap. The represented co-participant, Kayla, holds facts you would love to have — including that Jasmine never sprayed — and the temptation to contact her directly is real, but she is represented and the rule is a hard constraint. The identification and the school questioning invite scrutiny. Jasmine believes she was singled out because of a prior dress-code complaint against the identifying administrator, and she was questioned at school without a parent or lawyer — both worth examining. Restitution is a live number and a lever. The largest damage figure comes from the keyed car, which the eyewitness attributes to Andre; how you frame Jasmine's apportioned share matters. And collateral risk lurks: the phone video and the incomplete group-chat thread could each cut both ways, so how you counsel Jasmine about honesty in any accountability process is central.

08

Substantive Information

This is a California juvenile-delinquency matter, and your legal research should orient around several bodies of law that you will locate and read yourself (the packet states no citations by design; California juvenile procedure runs under the Welfare & Institutions Code, not the adult Penal Code process). Juvenile delinquency procedure. Delinquency proceedings in the Juvenile Division are not adult criminal prosecutions; they proceed by a wardship petition rather than a complaint, identify the child by initials, keep records confidential, and balance the minor's best interests with public safety and accountability. Understand the stages — petition, first appearance/detention, jurisdictional hearing, and disposition — and the standard of proof that applies at the jurisdictional hearing. The underlying offense. Research the elements of vandalism (including how the amount of damage is determined and what mental state must be shown), and how the law treats a person who is merely present, or who records conduct, versus one who participates or aids and abets. Pay attention to how presence, encouragement, and recording bear on liability. Diversion. Research how informal juvenile diversion works in California — eligibility, the effect of successful completion on the record, whether and how responsibility must be acknowledged, and what happens on non-completion. Statements and identification. Consider the rules governing questioning of a minor at school by an administrator and a resource officer without a parent or counsel present, how that bears on any statements, and the reliability and suggestiveness concerns raised by a single identifying administrator with a prior conflict. Professional responsibility. Study the rule governing communication with represented persons (California's analog to Model Rule 4.2), the confidentiality and privilege rules when a parent participates in conversations with a minor client, and the special duties owed to a minor client. Restitution and disposition factors. Finally, understand juvenile restitution and the non-legal inputs a juvenile court weighs — school engagement, first-offender status, family circumstances — because your disposition strategy and your counseling both depend on them.
