01

Introduction

You are an associate at Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP. Your client is Cloverdyke Dairy Cooperative, a farmer-owned dairy co-op near Danforth, in Voss County, Meridian. The co-op operates a shared station where member farms' milk is received and cooled in a large bulk tank before it ships to a creamery. Last fall the co-op bought a new bulk milk-cooling unit from Brandt Refrigeration Systems, LLC for $96,000. Within its first two months, the unit failed to hold temperature three times, spoiling three tank-loads of milk and forcing the co-op to pay for emergency repairs and a rented portable cooler. Brandt says the co-op's own electrical service and lapsed maintenance caused the failures, and it points to a limited-remedy warranty that disclaims consequential damages like spoiled milk. The co-op's general manager, Nils Aaberg, is coming in for an initial interview. The parties' agreement requires mediation and then binding arbitration, so this is not a courtroom trial — it is a sale-of-goods, breach-of-warranty dispute headed toward ADR. Your job in this exercise is to interview the client, develop the facts, analyze the warranty and remedies problem, and counsel the co-op on its options.

07

Considerations

Several tensions make this matter genuinely hard, and your analysis should wrestle with them rather than wish them away:

  • Warranty versus performance. The agreement contains both an express performance specification (hold at or below 40°F; pull down to 38°F) and a narrow repair-or-replace remedy. How do a promised performance level and a limited remedy interact when the equipment keeps failing the promised performance?
  • Failure of essential purpose. A repair-or-replace remedy assumes repairs actually restore a working product. When the same essential function fails repeatedly, consider whether the limited remedy has failed of its essential purpose — and, if it has, what that does to the separate exclusion of consequential damages.
  • The consequential-damages exclusion. The spoiled milk, lost premiums, emergency repairs, and rental cooler are exactly the consequential losses the contract purports to exclude. Whether that exclusion holds — and whether it survives a failed limited remedy or an unconscionability challenge in a commercial setting — is the money question.
  • Mixed causation. Your own engineer says the cause is mixed: an install defect and undersizing, plus a real electrical-supply problem, plus intermittent coil fouling. Some of that is your client's responsibility. Candor with the client about this is part of the exercise.
  • The client's harder facts. Deferred maintenance, a pre-purchase warning about the panel, and a skipped commissioning test all cut against the co-op. How you surface and handle these — with the client and in strategy — matters.
  • Forum and posture. This is headed to mediation and binding arbitration, not a jury. Consider how that shapes leverage, cost, confidentiality, and the realistic value of the claim.
08

Substantive Information

This is a sale-of-goods dispute governed by the law of Meridian for the sale of goods (the Uniform Commercial Code as adopted in Meridian). You are expected to research the governing law yourself; the following orients you to the terrain without doing the research for you.

The transaction is a sale of goods between merchants: a dairy cooperative buying commercial refrigeration equipment from a dealer-installer. Consider what express warranties the agreement created — including the written limited warranty and the express performance specification — and whether an implied warranty of merchantability or an implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose is in play, and whether any were effectively disclaimed.

The heart of the matter is remedies. A seller may contractually limit a buyer's remedy to repair or replacement, and may attempt to exclude consequential damages. But the law treats those as two distinct provisions. Research when a limited or exclusive remedy fails of its essential purpose, and what happens to the buyer's other remedies when it does. Research separately whether an exclusion of consequential damages is enforceable, how it is tested (including any unconscionability analysis), and whether a commercial buyer's consequential-damages exclusion stands or falls when the limited remedy has failed.

Also research the buyer's burden on breach and causation, the effect of the buyer's own maintenance obligations, the significance of the disputed commissioning, and the measure of damages for a buyer, including incidental and consequential damages and how spoiled inventory and cover-type costs are proven. Finally, orient yourself to the posture: the parties agreed to mediation and then binding arbitration. Consider what that means for the statement of the case, the standard of review, discovery, cost, and confidentiality, and how a mediation strategy differs from preparing to arbitrate. Bring the law to your written analysis; keep your client-facing communications facts-and-advice, not citations.
