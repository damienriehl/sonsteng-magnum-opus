## 1. Model Case Analysis (keyed to fact anchors)

### The organizing principle: the CDL, not the jail, is the case

The single most important thing an A-grade student does is keep the **commercial-license consequence at the center of the entire representation**. Wenzloff holds a Class A CDL and drives a company service truck daily — the credential *is* his employability [m15.fact.002], and a technician who loses his CDL cannot keep the job [m15.fact.037]. For a commercial driver, a first impaired-driving disqualification takes the CDL for **one year** and a second takes it **for life** (Minn. Stat. ch. 171 disqualification provisions) `[instructor: verify before teaching]`. Because of a prior impaired-driving offense about seven years back [m15.fact.027], a second disqualification is live — and that prior is why the State charged **Third-Degree DWI** rather than the lowest level [m15.fact.028]. The client's overriding, explicitly non-legal goal is to keep the CDL and the job, protect the family income, and stay out of jail [m15.fact.044] — in that order. A student who litigates this as a garden-variety first-offense DWI has misunderstood the matter.

### Two parallel proceedings on two clocks

The A-answer separates the **criminal charge** (Third-Degree DWI, Minn. Stat. § 169A.20 et seq.; degrees at §§ 169A.24–.27) `[instructor: verify before teaching]` from the **civil implied-consent license revocation**, which runs on its own clock through the implied-consent process (Minn. Stat. §§ 169A.51–.53) `[instructor: verify before teaching]` [m15.fact.029]. The implied-consent judicial-review petition has a **short filing window** — the defense already filed a timely petition [m15.fact.034] — and a top student flags why the clocks matter: the civil case can move faster and, depending on strategy, can be the first battleground for the license the client cares about most.

### The four defense threads [m15.fact.035]

**Thread 1 — Reasonable suspicion for the stop (c02.s01).** The stop rests on (a) an identified citizen's 911 weaving report (Corinne Vandersteen [m15.fact.013]) and (b) the deputy's own corroboration — following for ~1.5 miles and observing the truck touch the fog line **once** [m15.fact.014], [m15.fact.015]. The A-answer researches the tip-plus-corroboration analysis (Fourth Amendment; Minn. Const. art. I, § 10; an identified citizen informant is more reliable than an anonymous tip; see the anonymous-tip/weaving line of authority, e.g., Navarette v. California, 572 U.S. 393 (2014)) `[instructor: verify before teaching]`. The vulnerability the student must spot: Vandersteen was two or three cars back and **is not certain the stopped truck is the one she watched** [m15.fact.036], and the deputy corroborated only a **single** fog-line touch over a mile and a half — arguably thin. The dashcam [m15.fact.039] and the dispatch timestamps (call 10:50, stop 10:58 [m15.fact.042]) are the objective checks. (Caution: the *good facts* here are in tension with the client's admission — see below.)

**Thread 2 — Field-sobriety-test reliability (c02.s02).** The SFSTs (HGN, walk-and-turn, one-leg-stand [m15.fact.019]) were run on an icy shoulder at ~4° below zero, in wind, on packed snow and ice [m15.fact.017], after Wenzloff **asked to do them elsewhere and was refused** [m15.fact.018], by an exhausted man at the end of a ~14-hour double [m15.fact.005] with a reflux condition [m15.fact.010]. Fatigue, extreme cold, and reflux each undercut how much the balance tests can carry [m15.fact.020]. Governing framework: foundation/reliability under Minn. R. Evid. 702 `[instructor: verify before teaching]`; the body-worn-camera footage [m15.fact.039] is decisive evidence of the conditions.

**Thread 3 — The breath test / mouth-alcohol + margin of error (c02.s02).** The DataMaster read **0.09** [m15.fact.025] — just over the 0.08 per-se line. Two independent attacks: (a) the **15-minute continuous observation period** — it is an open question whether the operator (Sgt. Petrossian) actually watched Wenzloff without interruption for the full period [m15.fact.023]; and during that window he **burped and felt reflux come up**, with recent mouthwash and alcohol-based cough syrup still in play [m15.fact.024], [m15.fact.008], [m15.fact.009] — a classic **mouth-alcohol** contamination theory; and (b) **measurement uncertainty** — the DataMaster's stated uncertainty could place the true value **below 0.08** [m15.fact.040]. The A-answer frames a suppression/exclusion theory on the observation-period foundation and preserves the sub-0.08 argument for both the per-se charge and the implied-consent case.

**Thread 4 — Negotiated resolution protecting the CDL (c04).** Failing suppression, the plea strategy treats the **CDL disqualification as the target variable**, not the fine or the jail number. Because the prior makes this Third-Degree and threatens a lifetime CDL bar, the negotiation hunts for a charge/disposition that avoids a *qualifying* impaired-driving conviction for CDL purposes (e.g., research whether a plea to a non-qualifying offense, or a specific disposition, avoids the disqualification trigger) `[instructor: verify before teaching]`. Leverage: the thin stop, the icy-shoulder SFSTs, the 0.09-with-uncertainty reading. Exposure: the prior, the mouthwash-showing-consciousness-of-drinking fact, and the uncounted third drink.

### The signature pedagogical tension (the client's bad-good facts)

The gated/concealed facts cut **both ways**, and the A-answer counsels around them rather than burying them:
- The **fatigue-and-phone distraction** [m15.fact.012] gives an *innocent* explanation for the weaving (not alcohol) — but it **undercuts** a "the stop had no basis" argument (if he was genuinely drifting, the stop is easier to justify) and it is itself a driving-conduct problem. Double-edged.
- The **mouthwash to mask the beer smell** [m15.fact.008] and the **uncounted third drink** [m15.fact.007] are **bad** for the impairment/consciousness-of-guilt picture but **good** for the **mouth-alcohol** breath-test theory (recent oral alcohol). The A-student turns the embarrassing facts into breath-test ammunition while advising the client on their exposure.

### The A-grade conclusion

Push suppression hard on the **breath-test observation-period/mouth-alcohol** theory (the strongest, because it directly threatens the 0.09 and pairs with the client's own mouthwash/cough-syrup/reflux facts) and on the **thin stop** — while candidly weighing that the distraction admission complicates the stop challenge. Run the implied-consent petition on its own clock to protect the license. Whatever the suppression outcome, drive every negotiation toward a disposition that **avoids the CDL disqualification trigger** and keeps the client working — because that, not jail, is the catastrophe he retained counsel to prevent [m15.fact.030], [m15.fact.044].

## 4. Grading Guidance

### A / B / C discriminators
- **A (300–316.5):** Keeps the CDL consequence central to every decision; earns all four gated/concealed facts **including the prior DWI** without pressure; builds the strongest suppression theory (breath-test observation-period/mouth-alcohol, paired with the client's own mouthwash/cough-syrup/reflux facts); separates and times the two proceedings; counsels honestly on the double-edged facts; negotiates toward avoiding the disqualification trigger; flawless Rule 4.2 handling with the deputy and awareness of flat-fee scope.
- **B (268–285):** Solid issue-spotting and a competent interview; spots Rule 4.2; but treats the case as an ordinary first-offense DWI (underweighting the CDL/lifetime-bar stakes), misses or under-earns the prior, or fails to see the mouthwash/third-drink facts as breath-test ammunition.
- **C (~220):** Litigates jail exposure instead of the license; misses the prior entirely; conflates the criminal and implied-consent proceedings; contacts the deputy directly; or pressures the client and gets nothing.

### Common errors (watch for these)
1. **Contacting Deputy Kallestad directly** — the signature ethics failure (c05). Route through the prosecutor.
2. **Treating this as an ordinary first-offense DWI** — missing that the CDL, the prior, and the lifetime-disqualification risk are the whole matter.
3. **Never earning the prior DWI** — defending blind on the single fact that makes the charge Third-Degree and the license consequence catastrophic.
4. **Burying the bad facts instead of counseling around them** — not seeing that the mouthwash and third drink, while embarrassing, power the mouth-alcohol breath-test theory.
5. **Conflating the two proceedings** — missing that the implied-consent revocation runs on its own clock with a short petition window.
6. **Over-promising** — guaranteeing suppression or a saved license despite the 0.09 and the prior.
7. **Ignoring the tension** in the distraction admission — asserting "no basis for the stop" while the client admits he was genuinely drifting.

### Re-write demands (trigger a mandatory revision if)
- The student **contacted the deputy directly** — require a written Rule 4.2 analysis and a corrected plan routed through the county attorney.
- The analysis **treats the case as a simple first-offense DWI** and ignores the CDL/prior stakes — require a corrected strategy centered on the disqualification.
- The student **conflates the criminal charge with the implied-consent revocation** — require a corrected two-track analysis with the clocks.
- The negotiation plan **targets jail/fine rather than the CDL disqualification** — require a re-centered plea strategy.
