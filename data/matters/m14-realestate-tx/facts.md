# Ground-Truth Narrative — In re Purchase of the Cimarron Tract (Whitfield–Nakamura) {#b:1f2f98e5}

*Master facts for m14. This file is the single source of truth. Every material fact carries a sequential anchor `[m14.fact.NNN]`. Student-facing packets and personas draw only from these facts; nothing here states controlling law.* {#b:7bafc2a8}

## The buyer and the plan {#b:2b90cf64}

Kenji Nakamura is a forty-one-year-old site-reliability engineer who spent most of his career at a data-center operator in a Texas metro area [m14.fact.001]. In 2024 he began looking for rural acreage where he could run a modest cow-calf cattle operation and, on the flatter western pasture, host a utility-scale solar array under a ground lease [m14.fact.002]. He wants both because the cattle keep the grazing exemption alive while the solar lease supplies steady income [m14.fact.003]. {#b:387d3484}

Nakamura found the Cimarron Tract, a roughly 320-acre parcel of open rangeland on the Edwards Plateau in Edwards County, Texas, listed for sale in the fall of 2025 [m14.fact.004]. The tract's western third is level, cleared pasture that would suit a solar array; the eastern two-thirds is broken cedar and grazing land with a single set of pens and a water well near the old building site [m14.fact.005]. {#b:a1ec84d9}

The seller is Garrett Whitfield, a sixty-three-year-old rancher whose family has run the surrounding country for three generations [m14.fact.006]. Whitfield still lives on an adjacent homestead parcel that he is keeping; only the 320-acre Cimarron Tract is for sale [m14.fact.007]. {#b:f42c1830}

## The contract {#b:85999972}

On November 14, 2025, Nakamura and Whitfield signed a written Farm and Ranch earnest-money contract for the Cimarron Tract at a purchase price of $928,000 [m14.fact.008]. Nakamura deposited $15,000 of earnest money with Lone Mesa Title Company as escrow agent [m14.fact.009]. The contract set an original closing date of February 27, 2026, and gave the buyer a feasibility and title-review period during which he could terminate [m14.fact.010]. As title problems surfaced, the parties signed a written amendment on February 20, 2026, extending closing to April 30, 2026 to allow time to resolve the access and reservation issues [m14.fact.011]. {#b:a3245b06}

The firm, Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP, represents Nakamura as buyer's counsel; the firm's client is Kenji Nakamura [m14.fact.012]. Whitfield is separately represented by his own attorney, Colton Faircloth of Faircloth Land Law, for the sale [m14.fact.013]. {#b:0419ae61}

## What the title work turned up {#b:c3ca4278}

Lone Mesa Title Company issued a title commitment on December 5, 2025, prepared by escrow and title officer Nadia Castellano [m14.fact.014]. The commitment listed three problems that now drive the negotiation. {#b:e72d8af7}

**First — the access easement.** The Cimarron Tract has no direct frontage on a public road; its only all-weather access is a caliche ranch road, locally called Cimarron Road, that leaves a county road and crosses the neighboring Sandoval parcel before reaching the tract's western gate [m14.fact.015]. The commitment referenced a recorded 1991 easement instrument that purports to grant access, but the instrument's metes-and-bounds description follows a route along a fence line that does not match where the road actually runs today [m14.fact.016]. As a result, the recorded easement may not cover the road Nakamura would actually use, and its validity is in doubt [m14.fact.017]. {#b:816248eb}

**Second — the severed mineral estate.** A 1978 deed in the chain reserved the mineral estate, which is now owned by Ocotillo Minerals, LP, a party unrelated to Whitfield [m14.fact.018]. Because Texas treats the mineral estate as dominant, the mineral owner retains the right to use as much of the surface as is reasonably necessary to develop the minerals — a right Nakamura's solar plan must accommodate [m14.fact.019]. There is no current drilling on the tract, and no lease from Ocotillo has been produced [m14.fact.020]. {#b:0b715efc}

**Third — the well reservation Whitfield now wants.** During the title review, Whitfield asked, for the first time, to reserve a surface easement around the existing water well near the old building site — the "Cimarron well" — so he can keep drawing water from it [m14.fact.021]. The original contract said nothing about reserving the well or any surface easement to the seller [m14.fact.022]. {#b:f5c6ed39}

## The survey {#b:ddd8c33e}

Nakamura ordered a boundary and improvement survey from licensed surveyor Rosalind Deloria, delivered December 18, 2025 [m14.fact.023]. Deloria's survey confirmed three things. The physical Cimarron Road lies roughly 60 to 110 feet north of the route described in the 1991 easement instrument, and for part of its length it crosses the Sandoval parcel outside any recorded easement [m14.fact.024]. The Cimarron well sits near the northwest corner of the level western pasture — inside the very footprint the solar developer wants for the array [m14.fact.025]. And an old pipeline runs underground from the well toward Whitfield's adjacent homestead, suggesting the well has been feeding Whitfield's own place [m14.fact.026]. {#b:c31520c6}

## The neighbor and the road {#b:1cbd404e}

Everett Sandoval owns the parcel the road crosses and has used and maintained Cimarron Road himself for decades to reach his back pastures [m14.fact.027]. Sandoval says the road has been used openly by the Cimarron Tract's owners for as long as he can remember, but he has never signed a written easement and resents being asked to now [m14.fact.028]. Sandoval is willing to grant a clean, surveyed access easement to Nakamura — but only in exchange for a cash payment and a promise that construction traffic will not tear up his cattle guards [m14.fact.029]. {#b:97019549}

## The parties' confidential priorities {#b:8b69d044}

**Nakamura's confidential side.** Nakamura has a signed option and ground-lease term sheet from a solar developer for roughly 180 acres of the western pasture, worth far more over its term than the price of the land, but the option requires him to deliver clear, unencumbered access and a buildable western pasture by March 31, 2026, or the developer can walk [m14.fact.030]. That deadline — not the extended April 30 closing — is Nakamura's real pressure point, and he does not want Whitfield to know how valuable the solar deal is [m14.fact.031]. Because of the solar upside, Nakamura would actually pay up to about $40,000 more, or absorb the cost of buying an easement from Sandoval, rather than lose the tract — but his stated position is that title problems should reduce the price [m14.fact.032]. Nakamura's financing is a farm-and-ranch loan that is fully approved but conditioned on the lender's title company insuring access without exception [m14.fact.033]. {#b:f33dde15}

**Whitfield's confidential side.** Whitfield wants to keep the Cimarron well because its underground pipeline still supplies stock water to his adjacent homestead, and replacing that water source would cost him thousands and months of drilling [m14.fact.034]. He has not told Nakamura that the well is his only reliable water for the homestead, because he thinks admitting it would let the buyer squeeze him [m14.fact.035]. Whitfield is also under a balloon payment on his own operating note that comes due in the summer of 2026, so he needs this sale to close and cannot afford to walk or to spend months in litigation over the easement [m14.fact.036]. Whitfield privately knows the road has always run where it runs now and that the 1991 description was sloppy, but he would rather the buyer solve the Sandoval problem than reopen old family history with a neighbor [m14.fact.037]. {#b:4b0a7baf}

## Where things stand {#b:549c3282}

As of the working date, the parties are not walking away — both want to close [m14.fact.038]. The open terms are: whether the deed will be a general or special warranty deed and how it treats the outstanding mineral reservation; whether and how Whitfield reserves a surface easement for the Cimarron well without blocking the solar footprint; how the access easement gets fixed, whether by a corrective easement, a new grant from Sandoval, or a suit to establish an easement; and whether the purchase price is adjusted to reflect who bears those cure costs [m14.fact.039]. {#b:e986b090}

Nakamura's counsel has also flagged a professional-responsibility point: Whitfield is represented by Faircloth, so buyer's counsel and the buyer must route communications through Faircloth rather than contacting Whitfield directly about the deal [m14.fact.040]. Early in the engagement Nakamura mentioned, in passing, that he and Whitfield had been "texting back and forth" directly about the well, which counsel needs to address before it becomes a problem [m14.fact.041]. {#b:76670b17}

The earnest money remains in escrow at Lone Mesa Title, and the feasibility period has been preserved by the February amendment, so Nakamura retains the right to terminate and recover his deposit if the access cannot be made insurable [m14.fact.042]. {#b:4007e553}
