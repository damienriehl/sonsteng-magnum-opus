# Ground-Truth Facts — m07 · Cloverdyke Dairy Cooperative v. Brandt Refrigeration Systems, LLC

*Master narrative and single source of factual truth for this matter. Every material fact carries a sequential anchor. Personas, exhibits, and the rubric reference these anchors. Jurisdiction: Meridian (Voss County). Simulation "today" is June 30, 2026. This file is confidential ground truth; the student never sees it.*

---

## The co-op and the purchase

Cloverdyke Dairy Cooperative is a farmer-owned dairy cooperative in Voss County, near Danforth, with roughly forty member farms that ship raw milk to a shared receiving and bulk-cooling station the co-op operates. [m07.fact.001] Nils Aaberg has been the co-op's general manager since 2019 and runs the day-to-day operation, reporting to a seven-member farmer board. [m07.fact.002] By early 2025 the station's original bulk-cooling system was more than twenty years old and failing intermittently, and the board voted to replace it. [m07.fact.003]

The co-op solicited bids from three commercial refrigeration vendors, and Brandt Refrigeration Systems, LLC, a regional commercial-refrigeration dealer and installer, submitted the winning proposal. [m07.fact.004] On September 10, 2025, the co-op and Brandt signed an equipment purchase agreement for a new bulk milk-cooling and refrigeration unit, at a total price of $96,000. [m07.fact.005]

The agreement attached a written limited warranty. It promised that Brandt would repair or replace defective parts for twenty-four months, expressly limited the buyer's remedy to repair or replacement, and expressly disclaimed all incidental and consequential damages, including spoiled product and lost production. [m07.fact.006] The agreement also stated an express performance specification: the unit would hold the bulk tank at or below 40°F and pull fresh incoming milk down to 38°F within a set time after each milking. [m07.fact.007] A separate maintenance schedule in the warranty packet required the co-op to clean the condenser coils quarterly and to keep the unit registered. [m07.fact.008] The agreement contained a dispute-resolution clause requiring the parties to mediate and then submit any unresolved dispute to binding arbitration. [m07.fact.009]

## Installation and the disputed startup

Brandt's crew delivered and installed the unit on October 15, 2025. [m07.fact.010] The manufacturer's manual called for a formal commissioning inspection and a monitored 48-hour test run before the unit was placed into production service. [m07.fact.011] Aaberg was under pressure to bring the station online quickly, because the fall production peak was arriving and the old system had already been pulled. [m07.fact.012] Whether the full 48-hour commissioning test was actually performed is sharply disputed between the parties. [m07.fact.013]

## The three failures

The unit failed to hold temperature three separate times within its first two months of operation. [m07.fact.014] On November 3, 2025, the unit lost temperature overnight and the bulk tank rose to roughly 55°F; about 6,200 pounds of milk had to be dumped as spoiled. [m07.fact.015] The co-op's milk-house operator, Reuben Tuvang, discovered the high-temperature alarm when he arrived that morning and found the tank warm. [m07.fact.016] On November 24, 2025, the unit's compressor tripped off and about 5,800 pounds of milk spoiled before the problem was caught. [m07.fact.017] On December 12, 2025, temperature drifted upward over a weekend and roughly 7,000 pounds of milk were downgraded and ultimately dumped. [m07.fact.018]

The co-op ships its milk to Larkspur Creamery under a supply contract that pays a quality premium for milk meeting temperature and bacteria standards. [m07.fact.019] Larkspur's receiving grader, Corinne Callahan, rejected each of the three affected loads, documented them, and issued dump tickets and quality-deduction credit memos. [m07.fact.020] Between spoiled-milk value, lost quality premiums, emergency repairs, and a rented portable cooling trailer the co-op brought in to keep operating, the co-op calculates roughly $28,000 in consequential losses, on top of the $96,000 unit. [m07.fact.021] The co-op paid about $6,500 in emergency repairs and about $9,000 to rent the portable cooling trailer out of its own operating funds. [m07.fact.022]

## Brandt's service calls and the causation fight

Brandt's field service technician, Darius Pelletier, responded to each of the three calls. [m07.fact.023] Over the visits he replaced a failed contactor, reset the control board, and cleaned the condenser coils. [m07.fact.024] Pelletier's written service reports noted a "possible incoming power irregularity" and that the "condenser coils fouled; recommend maintenance per schedule." [m07.fact.025]

Brandt's position is that the failures were not the unit's fault. Brandt contends the co-op's electrical service — an undersized three-phase panel with voltage sag on one leg — caused the compressor to trip, and that the co-op let the condenser coils foul by skipping scheduled maintenance. [m07.fact.026] Brandt therefore invokes the warranty: it says the buyer's only remedy is repair or replacement of parts, and that consequential damages like spoiled milk are excluded. [m07.fact.027]

The co-op's position is that the unit was defective from installation, that the same essential cooling function failed repeatedly, and that a limited repair-or-replace remedy that never actually restores a working cooler has failed of its essential purpose — so the co-op should be able to recover its real losses. [m07.fact.028]

## The engineer's mixed findings

Aaberg's warranty-claim demand letter went to Brandt on January 20, 2026, seeking replacement of the unit and payment of the spoilage and related losses. [m07.fact.029] Brandt's written response on February 5, 2026 denied any obligation for consequential damages and offered only to re-inspect and repair. [m07.fact.030]

The co-op retained an independent refrigeration engineer, Ingemar Rask, P.E., who inspected the installed unit on February 27, 2026. [m07.fact.031] Rask found that the condensing unit's refrigerant charge and expansion valve had been set improperly at installation, and that the unit as configured was marginally undersized for the co-op's peak cooling load. [m07.fact.032] Rask separately measured a voltage imbalance on one leg of the milk-house's three-phase panel. [m07.fact.033] The upshot is that causation is genuinely mixed and disputed: an installation/configuration defect and an electrical-supply problem both appear to have contributed. [m07.fact.034]

## Records and the two harder facts

In addition to the unit's own controller, Tuvang kept a handwritten milk-house temperature log, and it corroborates the timing of the three temperature excursions. [m07.fact.035] Whether the unit controller's internal temperature data logs were automatically overwritten or can still be recovered is not known to anyone at the co-op. [m07.fact.036]

Two facts cut against the co-op. First, in late November 2025 the co-op deferred a scheduled condenser and filter service because it was short-staffed that month, leaving the coils dirtier than the maintenance schedule allowed. [m07.fact.037] Second, several months before the purchase, a local electrician, Dale Ostrowski, had warned Aaberg that the station's electrical panel was undersized and aging and should be upgraded, and the board deferred that upgrade to save money. [m07.fact.038]

One fact the co-op has not disclosed to Brandt: to go live before the production peak, Aaberg personally told Brandt's installer to skip the manufacturer's monitored 48-hour commissioning test. [m07.fact.039]

On Brandt's side, an internal fact it has not volunteered is that Brandt's own installer had flagged, before shipment, that the chosen condensing unit was at the very edge of its rated capacity for the co-op's stated load, and Brandt shipped it anyway rather than upsize. [m07.fact.040] Brandt also does not actually know whether the voltage imbalance on the panel predated the compressor faults or resulted from them. [m07.fact.041]

## Posture

The co-op's board authorized pursuing mediation and, if needed, binding arbitration under the agreement, and retained Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP in March 2026 to represent the co-op as buyer's counsel. [m07.fact.042] The spoiled-milk episodes have also strained the co-op's supply relationship with Larkspur Creamery, which the board regards as its most important economic relationship. [m07.fact.043] No lawsuit has been filed; the matter is presently pre-arbitration, in the demand-and-investigation phase, as of June 30, 2026. [m07.fact.044]
