# Ground-Truth Facts — m02 (In re Petition for Disciplinary Action against Gregor A. Winterhalt, a Meridian Attorney)

*Master narrative. This is the authoritative account behind the case file, the personas, and the rubric. Every material fact carries a sequential anchor `[m02.fact.NNN]`. Student-facing documents remain facts-only; legal conclusions live in the instructor notes.*

## The respondent and his practice

Gregor A. Winterhalt is a solo practitioner in Danforth, the seat of Voss County, and has been licensed in Meridian for about twenty-two years, holding license number MER-041982 [m02.fact.001]. His is a high-volume, low-overhead general practice: probate and estate administration, small residential real estate, occasional minor personal-injury claims, and family matters for neighbors and their children [m02.fact.002]. For years the office ran on Winterhalt plus a single part-time legal assistant, Nadia Fenwick, who handled filing, calendaring, and the checkbook until she left the practice in the summer of 2024 [m02.fact.003]. Around Danforth he had a settled reputation as a capable, fair lawyer who was chronically disorganized about paperwork and slow to send letters [m02.fact.004]. Danforth is a small river-valley farming community, and Winterhalt was a familiar figure there — he staffed a free legal-aid clinic at the county library one evening a month for years and had drafted wills for a good share of the town's older residents [m02.fact.046].

## The Marchetti settlement and the trust account

In early 2023 Winterhalt agreed to represent Delia Marchetti in a personal-injury claim arising from a fall in an icy grocery-store parking lot [m02.fact.005]. He settled Marchetti's claim in February 2024 for forty-two thousand dollars [m02.fact.006]. The settlement draft was made payable jointly to Winterhalt and Marchetti, and he endorsed and deposited it [m02.fact.007]. Instead of depositing the funds into his client trust (IOLTA) account, Winterhalt deposited the entire forty-two thousand dollars into his office operating account, commingling client money with firm money [m02.fact.008]. After his one-third contingent fee of fourteen thousand dollars and a documented medical lien of five thousand five hundred dollars, Marchetti's net share came to twenty-two thousand five hundred dollars [m02.fact.009]. When the draft cleared in late February 2024, Winterhalt told Marchetti only that the payout would take "a few weeks" to process [m02.fact.047]. Winterhalt did not disburse that net share to Marchetti for roughly four months, finally paying her in June 2024 [m02.fact.010]. He never sent Marchetti a written settlement statement or accounting showing how the forty-two thousand dollars had been divided among fee, lien, and client [m02.fact.048].

During those four months the operating-account balance dipped below the twenty-two thousand five hundred dollars Winterhalt owed Marchetti on at least two occasions [m02.fact.011]. In April 2024, with the practice short of cash, Winterhalt used approximately three thousand one hundred dollars of the funds sitting in the operating account to cover his office rent and Fenwick's pay [m02.fact.012]. He replaced the money within a few weeks and ultimately paid Marchetti her full net share plus six hundred dollars in interest he added voluntarily [m02.fact.013]. The medical lien was paid in full and no lienholder ever declared a default [m02.fact.014]. In the end, Marchetti lost no money [m02.fact.015].

## The Osei estate and the unanswered calls

Separately, Harun Osei had retained Winterhalt in 2023 to administer his late mother's estate through the Probate Division [m02.fact.016]. Between March and June of 2024, Osei telephoned the office roughly eleven times seeking a status update, and Winterhalt returned none of the calls [m02.fact.017]. The estate faced no imminent court deadline during the silence, though a creditor-claim window needed timely attention [m02.fact.018]. Winterhalt resumed the estate work in July 2024 and closed the administration without financial loss to Osei or the estate [m02.fact.019]. Frustrated at having been ignored for months, Osei filed a complaint with the Meridian Office of Lawyer Conduct in September 2024 [m02.fact.020].

## The personal crisis behind the lapses

The lapses clustered in a single difficult stretch. In March 2024 Winterhalt was hospitalized for a cardiac event, had a stent placed, and was out of the office for about three weeks [m02.fact.021]. The hospitalization landed on top of a divorce that had finalized in January 2024, and he sank into a depression [m02.fact.022]. For weeks that spring he simply stopped opening his mail and returning phone calls, letting correspondence and messages pile up unread [m02.fact.023]. He also began drinking heavily to cope through 2024; he later stopped and has attended a weekly recovery support group since October 2024 [m02.fact.024]. Compounding everything, a February 2024 migration of his practice-management software corrupted his trust-accounting ledger and destroyed his historical reconciliation data [m02.fact.025]. With no working ledger and, after Fenwick's departure, no staff to keep the books, Winterhalt lost track of what he was holding and for whom [m02.fact.026]. Fenwick had twice flagged for him, before she left, that the trust-account numbers no longer matched the client balances she could reconstruct, but he was out on medical leave and the warnings went unaddressed [m02.fact.049].

## The complaint, the investigation, and the petition

Marchetti eventually retained new counsel, attorney Lorna Beckwith, who sent Winterhalt a written demand for the funds and reported the matter to the Office of Lawyer Conduct in July 2024 [m02.fact.027]. The Office opened an investigation in August 2024 and consolidated the Marchetti and Osei complaints [m02.fact.028]. A Director's investigator interviewed Winterhalt in person in Danforth in October 2024, and he answered the questions without a lawyer present [m02.fact.050]. Winterhalt cooperated with the investigation, produced his bank records, and admitted that he had commingled the settlement funds [m02.fact.029]. In December 2024 he submitted to the Office a written remediation plan describing the new bookkeeper, the new trust account, and his monthly reconciliation routine [m02.fact.051]. He had not reported the trust problem to anyone before the two complaints surfaced it [m02.fact.030]. In January 2026 the Director filed a Petition for Disciplinary Action alleging violations relating to safekeeping of client property, diligence, and communication [m02.fact.031]. The Petition seeks a suspension and characterizes Winterhalt's temporary use of the operating-account funds as misappropriation [m02.fact.032]. The matter is proceeding before a Lawyers Professional Responsibility Board hearing panel with a referee, the Hon. Ingrid Solvang, appointed by the Meridian Supreme Court [m02.fact.033]. Final authority over any sanction rests with the Meridian Supreme Court [m02.fact.034].

## Remediation and record

Winterhalt did not stand still after the complaints. In November 2024 he hired a part-time bookkeeper, Curtis Ondracek, to take over the practice's finances [m02.fact.035]. He opened a proper IOLTA trust account and now performs a monthly three-way reconciliation [m02.fact.036]. He completed a trust-accounting continuing-legal-education course in December 2024 [m02.fact.037]. He has deliberately reduced his caseload and referred several matters to other lawyers to make the workload sustainable [m02.fact.038]. His disciplinary record is otherwise clean, except for a private admonition in 2019 for a missed filing deadline [m02.fact.039]. It is not yet settled whether that 2019 admonition will be treated as prior discipline in the present proceeding, and Winterhalt himself is unsure how it will be counted [m02.fact.040].

## The engagement and what is at stake

Winterhalt retained Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP in November 2025 to defend the disciplinary petition [m02.fact.041]. At fifty-eight, he fears losing his license and the livelihood he has built [m02.fact.042]. He is deeply embarrassed and reluctant to talk about the drinking and about having borrowed from the client funds, treating both as private failures rather than parts of his defense [m02.fact.043]. His goal is to persuade the panel and the Court to impose a public reprimand with a period of probation rather than a suspension [m02.fact.044]. He understands, and fears, that if the temporary use of Marchetti's funds is characterized as intentional misappropriation, the presumptive sanction could be disbarment [m02.fact.045].
