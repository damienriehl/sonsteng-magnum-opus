# Ground-Truth Facts — *In re Purchase of 4400 Kestrel Point, Sable County (Renard–Meskin)*

## The parties and the parcel

Talbot Meskin is a small residential developer who has spent the last decade building and renting out lake cabins around Sable County, in the State of Meridian [m04.fact.001]. He wants to buy a lakefront parcel known as 4400 Kestrel Point, on the north shore of Lake Verdant, and build four rental cabins on it [m04.fact.002]. The seller is Ruth Renard, a seventy-nine-year-old widow who has owned the 3.1-acre parcel since 1981 [m04.fact.003].

The parcel is beautiful but awkward: its only vehicle access is a gravel lane that crosses the neighboring parcel at 4380 Kestrel Point, which is owned by Corwin Delahaye [m04.fact.004]. Locals call the lane Birchrun Lane; it runs roughly six hundred feet from the county road down to the Renard parcel and has been used, in some form, for decades [m04.fact.005].

## The access problem

The whole deal turns on what, exactly, gives 4400 the right to use Birchrun Lane. A 1978 instrument, recorded with the Sable County Recorder, grants "a right of way for ingress and egress," but the wording is thin and ambiguous [m04.fact.006]. It is genuinely unclear whether that 1978 instrument created an easement appurtenant — a permanent access right that runs with the land and would pass to Meskin — or merely a revocable license, a neighborly permission personal to Ruth Renard that a landowner could pull [m04.fact.007].

The instrument was signed by Wendell Delahaye, Corwin's late father, and named "Harold and Ruth Renard" as the persons who could use the lane [m04.fact.008]. Harold Renard, Ruth's husband, died in 2019, and Ruth has owned the parcel solely since [m04.fact.009]. Corwin Delahaye, now fifty-four, inherited 4380 Kestrel Point in 2016 when Wendell died, and he contends that the lane was always a neighborly permission between two friends, not a permanent easement [m04.fact.010].

Making matters worse, although the 1978 instrument was recorded, it references only "the existing cartway" and contains no metes-and-bounds legal description of where the lane actually sits [m04.fact.011]. A licensed land surveyor, Nadia Brummond, walked and shot the property in March 2026 and found that the traveled gravel lane deviates roughly fifteen feet from where the vague 1978 description would place it [m04.fact.012]. As traveled, the lane actually runs closer to Delahaye's cabin than the instrument suggests, which is exactly what irritates Delahaye about the increased traffic four cabins would bring [m04.fact.013].

## The platted road and the city

There is a second, stranger access question. The 1971 plat of the "Kestrel Point Shores" subdivision shows a platted but never-built shoreline road, Verdant Shore Road, running along the rear (lake-side) of 4400 [m04.fact.014]. The City of Brightwater, the Sable County seat, has now issued a notice of a proposed vacation of the unbuilt portion of Verdant Shore Road [m04.fact.015]. If the city vacates that strip, the vacated land would ordinarily revert to the abutting owners — which could give 4400 a usable second access point, or could accomplish nothing useful, depending on exactly how the reversion falls and what the county's shoreland rules allow [m04.fact.016]. The vacation is on the Brightwater City Council's agenda for a public hearing in April 2026 [m04.fact.017]. Harlan Vestergaard, the Brightwater city planner, has explained that the city's motive is simply to shed maintenance liability on old platted-but-unbuilt roads, not to help or hurt any particular owner [m04.fact.018].

## The listing and the negotiation so far

The listing broker, Roland Achleitner, put 4400 Kestrel Point on the market in January 2026 at an asking price of $415,000 [m04.fact.019]. Meskin made an initial written offer of $360,000 on February 10, 2026 [m04.fact.020]. The seller's property disclosure that Achleitner prepared described the access as "a shared driveway easement," which arguably overstates how settled the access right really is [m04.fact.021]. After Meskin's counsel raised the ambiguity, Renard's counsel walked the language back, characterizing the access more cautiously as "a longstanding permissive use" [m04.fact.022].

There is also a density question hanging over the whole plan: Meskin's four-cabin proposal depends on getting that unit count approved, and Sable County's shoreland-district rules may cap development on the parcel at fewer than four units [m04.fact.023]. The transaction is being negotiated toward a purchase agreement rather than litigated, and each side is holding confidential facts the other does not know [m04.fact.038]. Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP represents Meskin, the buyer; Ruth Renard is represented by separate counsel [m04.fact.039]. The title company's commitment lists the ambiguous 1978 access instrument as a Schedule B exception, so title insurance will not cover a failure of access unless the defect is cured before closing [m04.fact.040]. The options on the table are to obtain a clean recorded easement from Delahaye, to condition closing on the outcome of the city road vacation, or to cut the price to reflect the access risk [m04.fact.041]. Meskin retained the firm on February 18, 2026, to negotiate and paper the deal [m04.fact.042].

## Confidential to the buyer (Meskin's side)

Meskin has a signed letter of intent from a vacation-rental management company that is contingent on having four operational cabins ready by the summer of 2027 [m04.fact.024]. His construction financing is approved, but the commitment expires if he does not close on the purchase by August 15, 2026 [m04.fact.025]. Although his written offer was $360,000, Meskin is privately willing to pay as much as $395,000 to get the parcel [m04.fact.026]. He has also quietly learned that Delahaye himself approached Achleitner about buying 4400 — apparently to keep it undeveloped [m04.fact.027]. Finally, a preliminary soil test suggests that the best cabin sites will require an engineered septic system, adding roughly $60,000 to his development cost [m04.fact.028].

## Confidential to the seller (Renard's side)

Ruth Renard has been diagnosed with early-stage congestive heart failure and intends to move into an assisted-living residence near her daughter [m04.fact.029]. She needs the sale proceeds to fund the entrance fee at that residence, which makes closing this year important to her [m04.fact.030]. Her late husband Harold always told her the lane was "ours by right," but she has no personal memory of the terms of the 1978 arrangement and cannot say what was actually agreed [m04.fact.031]. She also holds a backup offer of $348,000 from a retiree couple who want the land as-is and would not develop it [m04.fact.032]. And she is genuinely worried that a fight with Corwin Delahaye — whose father was Harold's close friend — will poison the neighborhood she has loved for four decades [m04.fact.033].

## The neighbor (Delahaye)

Corwin Delahaye would strongly prefer that 4400 stay undeveloped, because four rental cabins would send far more traffic past his home on the narrow lane [m04.fact.034]. He insists that his father Wendell only ever gave Harold Renard "permission to use the two-track," never a permanent right of any kind [m04.fact.035]. Delahaye has, for years, maintained and plowed the gravel lane at his own expense, which he views as further proof it was always his to control [m04.fact.036]. Delahaye has floated two possibilities: he would sell Ruth (or a buyer) a properly recorded easement for $25,000, or he would simply buy 4400 outright himself [m04.fact.037].
