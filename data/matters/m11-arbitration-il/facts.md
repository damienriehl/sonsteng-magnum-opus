# Ground-Truth Facts — *Prairie State Logistics, Inc. v. Yusuf Adeyemi* (Employment Arbitration, Illinois)

*Master narrative for the m11 matter. This is the single source of truth; every persona, exhibit, and rubric evidence pointer resolves to an anchor below. Student-facing packets are facts-only.*

## The employee and the workplace

Yusuf Adeyemi was hired by Prairie State Logistics, Inc. in March 2019 as a warehouse associate and was promoted to second-shift operations supervisor in 2022, giving him roughly seven years with the company by early 2026 [m11.fact.001]. Prairie State operates a third-party logistics distribution center in the Interstate 80 corridor near Joliet, in Will County, Illinois, with about 180 employees across three shifts [m11.fact.002]. As second-shift supervisor, Adeyemi ran a crew of about two dozen inbound and outbound workers [m11.fact.003]. His record was strong: positive annual reviews from 2022 through 2024, an "exceeds expectations" rating in 2023, and a spot bonus in 2024 [m11.fact.004].

## The unpaid-time and safety problems

Beginning in the summer of 2025, site management pushed hard to hit rising outbound quotas, and Adeyemi's crew was regularly expected to keep working after they had clocked out [m11.fact.005]. A timekeeping-system change that year automatically clocked employees out at their scheduled shift end, so any work performed afterward went unrecorded and unpaid — off-the-clock labor [m11.fact.006]. In September 2025, Adeyemi raised the unpaid-time problem verbally with his shift manager and the site's director of operations, Roland Pfeiffer, who told him to "make the numbers work" [m11.fact.007].

During the October–November 2025 peak season, fire exits in the outbound bay were repeatedly blocked by staged pallets [m11.fact.008]. On November 5, 2025, Adeyemi photographed a blocked north fire exit on his phone [m11.fact.009].

## Adeyemi reports, internally and to the state

On November 10, 2025, Adeyemi sent a written complaint by email to Bethany Croll, the site's HR business partner, describing both the off-the-clock work and the blocked exits [m11.fact.010]. Croll acknowledged the email on November 12, 2025, and said she would "look into it" [m11.fact.011]. Receiving no substantive response, Adeyemi filed a complaint with a state wage-and-safety hotline on November 18, 2025, and received a confirmation number [m11.fact.012].

What Adeyemi did not know at the time is that on November 12, 2025 — the same day she acknowledged his email — Croll forwarded his internal complaint to Pfeiffer, the very manager Adeyemi had accused [m11.fact.027]. HR's internal notes reflecting that forward were produced later in the arbitration.

## The turn against him

On December 5, 2025, Pfeiffer placed Adeyemi on a written Performance Improvement Plan (PIP) citing "outbound accuracy" and "team coaching" deficiencies [m11.fact.013]. The PIP was the first negative document in Adeyemi's entire personnel file [m11.fact.014]. The metrics it cited covered October and November 2025 — the same weeks his crew had been working short-staffed and past clock-out [m11.fact.015].

On January 9, 2026, Prairie State terminated Adeyemi, citing both his failure to meet PIP goals and a "site headcount reduction" [m11.fact.016]. The separation notice listed both performance and a reduction-in-force, an overlapping and shifting set of rationales [m11.fact.017]. A reduction-in-force memo dated January 8, 2026 eliminated two site supervisor roles; Adeyemi was one, and the other was a supervisor already scheduled to retire [m11.fact.018]. Within six weeks of Adeyemi's termination, Prairie State posted a "shift operations lead" opening for the second shift [m11.fact.019].

At termination, the company offered Adeyemi four weeks' severance conditioned on signing a release of all claims; he refused to sign [m11.fact.039].

## The arbitration agreement

Adeyemi's employment agreement, signed in 2019 and reaffirmed at his 2022 promotion, contains a mandatory arbitration clause covering "all disputes arising from employment," together with a class-action waiver [m11.fact.020]. The clause designates a private arbitral forum and Illinois law and provides that the employer pays the arbitrator's fee [m11.fact.021]. Whether that clause and its class waiver are fully enforceable is a legal question Adeyemi himself cannot answer [m11.fact.040].

## The dispute

Adeyemi believes the timing tells the story: the PIP landed about three-and-a-half weeks after his state hotline complaint, and the firing came roughly two months after — a sequence he reads as retaliation for reporting wage and safety violations [m11.fact.022]. He seeks reinstatement or front and back pay, recovery of unpaid off-the-clock wages, and a neutral employment reference [m11.fact.031]. From crew logs he kept, Adeyemi estimates about 90 to 110 hours of unpaid off-the-clock time across his team over 2025 [m11.fact.032].

Prairie State's position, through Pfeiffer, is that the PIP reflected genuine accuracy problems and predated any knowledge of the hotline complaint — a claim the internal-notes timeline disputes [m11.fact.028]. The company also asserts it never received notice of the state hotline complaint until after the termination, which is likewise disputed by the documentary chronology [m11.fact.029].

## Corroboration

Tomás Ruvalcaba, a forklift operator and lead on Adeyemi's crew, confirms that the crew regularly worked twenty to forty minutes past clock-out without pay during October and November 2025 [m11.fact.023]. Ruvalcaba also heard Pfeiffer tell Adeyemi to "keep the belt running" after the scheduled shift end [m11.fact.024]. Ayana Osei, an inbound clerk, saw pallets blocking the north fire exit on multiple nights and heard Adeyemi instruct a lead to clear them [m11.fact.025]. Osei recalls that after Adeyemi's complaint, supervisors held a meeting warning staff not to "go outside the building" with their concerns [m11.fact.026].

## The client, up close

Adeyemi is the family's primary earner; the termination cost him both his income and his health coverage, and his wife had recently moved to reduced hours [m11.fact.030]. He is generally cooperative but anxious, and he tends to minimize anything that might reflect on him [m11.fact.038]. Two facts he holds close: in August 2025 he approved a subordinate's timecard edit to add fifteen minutes the system had cut — technically outside company policy — and he worries it could be turned against him [m11.fact.034]; and he kept a personal notebook logging late-work nights and who stayed, which he has not yet disclosed to anyone [m11.fact.033]. Quietly, in October 2025, he had applied to one other logistics company and was not hired; he is embarrassed about it and has told no one, fearing it makes him look like he was on his way out anyway [m11.fact.041]. His deepest fear is being blackballed across the regional logistics industry, where site managers all seem to know one another [m11.fact.035].

## The evidentiary picture

The claimant's case is largely circumstantial and turns on sequence and knowledge. The internal email chain shows that on the same morning Croll acknowledged Adeyemi's complaint, she forwarded it to Pfeiffer — the manager Adeyemi had named — so the very person who later authored the performance improvement plan and made the termination decision had the complaint in hand weeks before he acted [m11.fact.027]. The performance improvement plan, standing alone in an otherwise spotless seven-year file [m11.fact.014], cites accuracy metrics drawn from the exact weeks the crew was working short-staffed and unpaid past clock-out [m11.fact.015]. The timekeeping export corroborates that pattern: the system clocked the crew out automatically at the scheduled shift end while dock and badge logs show outbound work continuing twenty to forty minutes later on most sampled nights [m11.fact.006]. The employer, for its part, will lean on the reduction-in-force memo and the accuracy data to argue that the decisions were legitimate and independent of any protected activity [m11.fact.028], and will assert it had no notice of the external hotline complaint until after the termination [m11.fact.029]. Whether the two employer rationales — a for-cause performance failure and a neutral headcount reduction — reinforce or undercut one another is a central question, sharpened by the second-shift job posting that appeared within six weeks of Adeyemi's departure [m11.fact.019].

## Posture

Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP represents Adeyemi as claimant/employee; Prairie State Logistics is represented by outside employment counsel [m11.fact.036]. A demand for arbitration was filed in February 2026, with the hearing anticipated later in 2026 and pre-hearing document exchange ongoing as of June 30, 2026 [m11.fact.037]. Because the arbitration clause and its class waiver govern the forum [m11.fact.020] and assign the arbitrator's fee to the employer [m11.fact.021], the strategic picture differs from a court trial: a streamlined record, a single neutral deciding both fact and law, and a narrow path to any later court review. Settlement remains live — Adeyemi rejected the conditional severance release at termination [m11.fact.039], but a resolution that secured back pay, the unpaid off-the-clock wages he estimates for his crew [m11.fact.032], and above all a neutral reference could serve his real objectives [m11.fact.031] better than an all-or-nothing award.
