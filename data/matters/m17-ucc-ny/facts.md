## The buyer and the supply deal

Empire Provisions Corp. is a New York specialty-food distributor based in Newburgh, in the Hudson Valley, with about forty employees; it supplies shelf-stable specialty groceries — private-label jarred sauces, packaged grains, and bottled olive-oil products — to independent grocers and restaurants across the Northeast. [m17.fact.001] Marisol Beltran has been Empire's procurement director since 2021; she negotiates the company's co-packing supply contracts and reports directly to Empire's owner-president. [m17.fact.002] Ostervold Packing Co. is an upstate New York contract packer, near Utica, that produces private-label jarred and packaged foods for distributors like Empire. [m17.fact.003]

In the spring of 2025 Empire needed a seasonal co-packer for its private-label line and solicited Ostervold, which submitted the winning proposal. [m17.fact.004] On May 15, 2025, Empire and Ostervold signed a Master Supply Agreement for six truckloads of packaged specialty foods over the 2025 season, each load released by an individual purchase order at roughly $48,000 per load. [m17.fact.005]

The agreement set written product specifications. Each unit's label had to state an accurate net weight, carry a complete and accurate allergen and ingredient statement matching the approved artwork, and bear a printed "best by" date code in a specified format. [m17.fact.006] The agreement also required a minimum remaining shelf life on delivery: each case had to arrive with at least nine months before its printed "best by" date. [m17.fact.007] It required intact packaging and gave Empire a right to inspect and reject nonconforming goods on receipt, but it fixed no specific rejection deadline beyond acting "promptly." [m17.fact.008] Finally, the agreement provided that if Empire rejected a delivery, Ostervold could cure the nonconformity within a reasonable time. [m17.fact.009]

## The deliveries

Ostervold delivered the first load — Load A — on August 12, 2025; Empire inspected it, accepted it, and paid in full without incident. [m17.fact.010] Load B was delivered on September 3, 2025. [m17.fact.011] Empire's receiving manager, Tobias Vandermeer, inspected Load B and found that the jarred-sauce cases bore an allergen statement that omitted the "contains tree nuts" warning shown on the approved artwork. [m17.fact.012] Empire rejected Load B by written notice on September 5, 2025 and held the goods for Ostervold's account. [m17.fact.013]

Load C was delivered on September 22, 2025. [m17.fact.014] Its packaged-grain cases were printed with "best by" dates only about five months out — well short of the nine-month contractual minimum. [m17.fact.015] Because Empire's receiving dock was badly backed up during a peak shipping week, Load C sat largely uninspected in Empire's warehouse for roughly eight days before Vandermeer caught the short dates. [m17.fact.016] Empire rejected Load C on September 30, 2025. [m17.fact.017]

Load D was delivered on October 6, 2025; it was a mixed load of jarred sauces and bottled olive-oil products. [m17.fact.018] Some of Load D's cases carried the same missing tree-nut warning and some bore short dates, while part of the load appeared to conform. [m17.fact.019] When Load D arrived, Empire was under pressure to fill a standing order from a downstream grocery customer, and Beltran authorized moving about a third of Load D's pallets directly into that outbound customer shipment before the load had been fully inspected. [m17.fact.020] Empire rejected the remainder of Load D on October 8, 2025. [m17.fact.021]

## The demand, the cure offer, and the suit

Ostervold's plant QA manager, Curtis Ferraro, kept production and shipping records showing lot codes and production dates for each load. [m17.fact.022] Ostervold disputed the rejections, asserting that the labels matched artwork Empire's own staff had approved and that any date issue was within tolerance. [m17.fact.023] On October 15, 2025, Ostervold sent Empire a letter offering to cure — to pick up the rejected goods, relabel or replace them, and redeliver within three weeks. [m17.fact.024] Empire declined the cure offer on October 20, 2025, stating that it had lost confidence in Ostervold's quality control and had already sourced replacement product elsewhere. [m17.fact.025]

Ostervold then invoiced Empire the full contract price for Loads B, C, and D — a total of about $147,000 — and demanded payment. [m17.fact.026] Empire refused to pay for the three disputed loads. [m17.fact.027] Ostervold filed suit against Empire on February 10, 2026 in the Supreme Court of the State of New York, Commercial Division, Westchester County, seeking the contract price for the three loads. [m17.fact.028] Ostervold's complaint alleges that the goods conformed, that Empire's rejections were untimely, and that Empire accepted and resold part of the goods, so Empire owes the price. [m17.fact.029]

## Conformity and causation disputes

Empire retained an independent food-labeling and safety consultant, Priyanka Sengupta, who examined retained samples on April 14, 2026. [m17.fact.030] Sengupta confirmed that Load B and part of Load D bore an allergen statement omitting the tree-nut warning shown on the approved artwork — a genuine labeling nonconformity. [m17.fact.031] She also confirmed that Load C's date codes reflected under six months of remaining shelf life on the delivery date, short of the contractual nine months. [m17.fact.032] Ostervold contends that the short dating resulted partly from slow freight transit and Empire's own delay in inspecting, not from late production. [m17.fact.033] Whether the short shelf life originated at Ostervold's production line or was consumed by transit and Empire's eight-day inspection lag is genuinely disputed. [m17.fact.034] Freight broker Yosef Mizrahi's carrier records document the delivery dates and, for Loads B and C, the later pickup of the rejected goods by Ostervold's carrier. [m17.fact.035]

## Records and the harder facts

Vandermeer's receiving logs and rejection notices corroborate the delivery dates and the defects he found, but they also show that Load C sat about eight days before it was inspected. [m17.fact.036] Empire's inventory and resale records show that the Load D pallets Beltran moved to the grocery customer were shipped out and sold, not returned to inventory or to Ostervold. [m17.fact.037] During this same period, Empire had been quietly planning to shift its private-label volume to a cheaper competing co-packer, and Beltran had been told by Empire's owner to reduce the company's reliance on Ostervold. [m17.fact.038] Beltran does not actually know whether Ostervold produced Load C late or whether its short dating was consumed in transit. [m17.fact.039]

## Ostervold's side

Ostervold's internal production notes reflect a labeling-line changeover mid-season, during which at least one lot ran with an outdated allergen template — a nonconformity Ostervold has not volunteered. [m17.fact.040] Ostervold also ran a production backlog in September that pushed some lots to later production dates with less remaining shelf life than the purchase orders required. [m17.fact.041] Ostervold genuinely does not know whether Empire truly resold the moved Load D pallets or merely shifted them within its own warehouse. [m17.fact.042]

## Posture

Empire's owner authorized defending the price action and asserting rightful-rejection and breach-of-warranty defenses, and retained Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP in March 2026 as buyer's counsel; the firm had represented Empire once before, in an unrelated closed matter, so Empire came in as a returning client. [m17.fact.043] Empire has answered the complaint, denied that the goods conformed, and pleaded rightful rejection; the parties are in discovery in the Commercial Division as of June 30, 2026, and no trial date has been set. [m17.fact.044]

The practical shape of the dispute is that the three loads are not equal. Load B presents the cleanest rejection for Empire, because the missing tree-nut warning is a documented labeling defect that Ostervold's own records partly corroborate. Load C presents a genuine short-dating defect but is clouded by the eight-day inspection delay and by the fight over whether the short life began at the plant or ran off the clock on Empire's floor. Load D is the hardest of the three: part of it may have conformed, part did not, and Empire's decision to move roughly a third of it to a customer before rejecting the rest is the fact most likely to expose Empire to paying at least part of the price. How buyer's counsel sorts these three loads — rather than treating them as one all-or-nothing event — is the heart of the matter.
