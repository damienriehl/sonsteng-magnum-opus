## 1. Model Case Analysis (keyed to fact anchors) {#b:58e5e03e}

### 1.1 Posture {#b:8eed9e02}
The firm represents **Empire (buyer/defendant)** [role `m17.role.buyer-counsel`] in Ostervold's filed action for the **contract price** of Loads B, C, and D (~$147,000) in the Supreme Court, Commercial Division, Westchester County [m17.fact.028]. The Master Supply Agreement (May 15, 2025) set express specs — accurate net weight, complete/accurate allergen + ingredient statement matching approved artwork, printed "best by" date code [m17.fact.006], a **nine-month minimum remaining shelf life on delivery** [m17.fact.007], intact packaging with a right to inspect and reject "promptly" (no fixed deadline) [m17.fact.008], and a **seller's right to cure** within a reasonable time [m17.fact.009]. The governing frame is perfect tender, rejection, acceptance, cure, and the seller's price-vs.-damages remedy under N.Y. U.C.C. Law Article 2 `[instructor: verify before teaching]`. **The core skill is reasoning load-by-load, not all-or-nothing.** {#b:863c29ef}

### 1.2 Load A — the baseline {#b:d3e2393f}
Conforming, inspected, accepted, paid [m17.fact.010]. Establishes Empire is not a reflexive rejecter — useful to rebut the pretext narrative. {#b:addaad4f}

### 1.3 Load B — the cleanest rejection for Empire {#b:27c9613b}
Allergen statement omitted the "contains tree nuts" warning shown on approved artwork [m17.fact.012] — a genuine express-warranty nonconformity (N.Y. U.C.C. § 2-313; approved artwork = the express spec) and a merchantability problem (§ 2-314) `[instructor: verify before teaching]`. Rejected **same-inspection**, by written notice Sept. 5 (delivered Sept. 3) [m17.fact.013], goods held for seller's account — a textbook rightful, seasonable rejection under §§ 2-601/2-602 `[instructor: verify before teaching]`. Ostervold's own QA log corroborates a mid-season label-template changeover [m17.fact.040]; Sengupta confirms [m17.fact.031]. **Model conclusion: Empire owes nothing on Load B; no price under § 2-709.** {#b:83ed7423}

### 1.4 Load C — genuine defect clouded by delay {#b:844fd8a0}
Date codes ~5 months vs. the 9-month spec [m17.fact.015], confirmed by Sengupta at under six months [m17.fact.032] — a real nonconformity. **But** the load sat ~8 days before inspection [m17.fact.016], [m17.fact.036], and rejection came Sept. 30 (delivered Sept. 22) [m17.fact.017]. The live issue is **timeliness**: rejection must be within a reasonable time with seasonable notice (§ 2-602); undue delay can operate as **acceptance** (§ 2-606(1)(b)) `[instructor: verify before teaching]`. A model answer argues eight days during a documented peak week may still be reasonable for a perishable-dating defect not visible without inspection, while candidly conceding the delay is Empire's exposure point. **Causation** is separately disputed: Ostervold blames transit + Empire's dock lag [m17.fact.033], but the carrier records [m17.fact.035] and Ostervold's own September production backlog [m17.fact.041] point to short-dating at the plant. Whether the short life originated in production or ran off on Empire's floor is genuinely open [m17.fact.034], and Beltran cannot herself resolve it [m17.fact.039]. {#b:996d9ab4}

### 1.5 Load D — the hardest; the acceptance-by-resale trap {#b:89e252fd}
Mixed load: some cases with the missing tree-nut warning, some short-dated, part apparently conforming [m17.fact.019]. Under pressure to fill a standing customer order, Beltran authorized moving ~1/3 of the pallets **directly into an outbound customer shipment before full inspection** [m17.fact.020]; those pallets were shipped and **sold**, not returned [m17.fact.037]. Reselling goods is an act inconsistent with the seller's ownership — **acceptance** under § 2-606(1)(c) — which triggers the buyer's obligation to pay at the contract rate for what was accepted (§ 2-607(1)) `[instructor: verify before teaching]`. Empire rejected the **remainder** Oct. 8 [m17.fact.021]. A model answer: Empire likely **owes the price for the resold ~1/3** (accepted by resale), while the properly rejected remainder turns on conformity and commercial-unit rules (§§ 2-105(6), 2-601(c)) — a buyer generally may not reject conforming commercial units, so the mixed character must be sorted, not treated wholesale. {#b:6a522431}

### 1.6 Cure and the seller's remedy {#b:08845bd3}
Ostervold offered to cure — pick up, relabel/replace, redeliver within three weeks [m17.fact.024]; Empire declined Oct. 20, citing lost confidence and replacement sourcing [m17.fact.025]. A model answer weighs § 2-508 (seller's cure right; stronger before the performance date, more limited after) `[instructor: verify before teaching]` and notes the refused cure cuts *for* Ostervold on any load where the rejection was shaky (esp. C) and matters little on Load B (clean rejection) — but a buyer who has genuinely lost confidence after repeated nonconformity has a real argument. **The price question (§ 2-709):** a seller recovers the **price** only for accepted goods, goods lost/damaged after risk passed, or goods not reasonably resalable [instructor: verify before teaching]. Rejected, resalable goods put the seller in **damages** (§ 2-706 resale / § 2-708 market differential), not price. So Ostervold's full-price claim likely fails except for the **resold Load D portion** (accepted) — the analytical payoff. {#b:0a666db6}

### 1.7 The pretext backdrop {#b:ef1ab96e}
Empire had been planning to shift volume to a cheaper packer, and the owner told Beltran to reduce reliance on Ostervold [m17.fact.038]. Even with real defects, opposing counsel will argue the rejections were pretextual. A model answer surfaces this candidly and shows the defects are documented and independently confirmed [m17.fact.031]–[m17.fact.032], so motive does not defeat a rightful rejection where the goods were in fact nonconforming — but it counsels the client on how it looks. {#b:b5b5e82d}

Frame § 2-709 price vs. § 2-706/§ 2-708 damages: Ostervold likely wins the **price only on the accepted (resold) Load D share** and is relegated to damages on rejected, resalable goods; address the mixed causation of the short-dating candidly; build a realistic exposure range and defense theory. {#b:d68e315b}

## 4. Grading Guidance {#b:a51dcb2b}

- **Load-by-load is the whole point.** The signature error is treating three deliveries as one all-or-nothing event. Top papers reach different conclusions per load (B: no price; C: contested on timeliness; D: price on the resold share) and say so. {#b:2f3aefe1}
- **Acceptance-by-resale is the trap that separates A from B.** A student who misses that moving ~1/3 of Load D to a customer is acceptance under § 2-606(1)(c) — and thus price owed for that portion — has missed the hardest and most important point. {#b:338115df}
- **Timeliness candor.** Credit students who confront the eight-day Load C gap honestly and argue reasonableness for a not-visible dating defect, rather than pretending the delay away. {#b:4c541539}
- **Price vs. damages.** Reward the § 2-709 analysis showing the seller cannot recover the full price on rejected, resalable goods — the doctrinal reward of the exercise. {#b:28b6b068}
- **Rule 4.2 bright line.** Any direct contact with Ostervold or his employees is a hard miss on c05, whatever it produced. Credit discovery/opposing-counsel routing instead. {#b:9ca19a4b}
- **Interview technique over extraction** and the **two-axis debrief** per `docs/research/interview-pedagogy.md`: score Axis A fact coverage (+ the Rule 4.2 flag) and Axis B relational experience in character; earning the hurtful facts through non-judgment and hint-following is the target, not a lucky reveal. {#b:51683d06}
