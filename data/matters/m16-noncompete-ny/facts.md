# Ground-Truth Facts — Hudson Valley Biometrics, Inc. v. Priya Iyer

*Master narrative for matter m16. This is the authoritative record. Every material fact carries a sequential anchor `[m16.fact.NNN]`. Student-facing documents state no law; the anchors below are the spine that personas, exhibits, and the rubric reference.*

## The company and the engineer

Hudson Valley Biometrics, Inc. ("HVB") is a computer-vision company headquartered in Poughkeepsie, in Dutchess County, New York, that develops face-and-gait recognition software sold mainly to law-enforcement and public-safety agencies [m16.fact.001]. Its flagship product is a person-matching system that turns video of a face or a walking gait into a numeric "embedding" and compares it against a watch-list database [m16.fact.002]. The pipeline that produces and compares those embeddings — which the company calls internally "the matching engine" — is the asset HVB treats as its most valuable property [m16.fact.003].

Priya Iyer is a senior algorithms engineer who joined HVB in 2019 and spent nearly seven years there, rising to technical lead on the matching engine [m16.fact.004]. She was widely regarded as one of the two or three people who understood the whole pipeline end to end [m16.fact.005]. On her first day in 2019, as a condition of employment, she signed an employment agreement containing three restrictive covenants: a non-competition clause, a non-solicitation clause, and a confidentiality/trade-secret clause [m16.fact.006]. The non-compete bars her, for two years after leaving, from working for "any business engaged in biometric identification, computer-vision matching, or related fields," anywhere, with no geographic limit and no carve-out for unrelated products [m16.fact.007]. She signed the packet in an onboarding session with roughly a dozen other new hires and never negotiated a word of it; the same form is given to every engineer, and no one is told it is negotiable [m16.fact.008].

## What the "matching engine" actually is

The heart of HVB's dispute is its claim that the matching engine is a protected trade secret that Priya cannot help but disclose. The reality is more mixed. The core techniques in the pipeline — the embedding architecture, the loss functions used to train it, the nearest-neighbor search that does the matching — are drawn substantially from published academic papers and widely used open-source machine-learning libraries [m16.fact.009]. HVB's engineers routinely discussed the architecture in all-hands meetings, and the source code lived in shared internal repositories that every member of the engineering team could read and check out [m16.fact.010]. There was no separate password, no "need to know" compartment, and no document consistently marked "trade secret" describing the engine as a whole [m16.fact.011]. What HVB has that is genuinely its own is the specific tuning: the particular hyperparameters, the curated training data, and the accumulated engineering judgment about what works — none of which Priya took in any tangible form [m16.fact.012].

## The departure

In the spring of 2025, Priya was passed over for a principal-engineer promotion she had been told for two years was coming, and a new engineering director was brought in over her whom she found dismissive and hostile [m16.fact.013]. She began quietly interviewing that autumn. In December 2025 she accepted an offer from Sightline Perception, Inc., a competitor [m16.fact.014]. Sightline builds gait-based access-control software — systems that recognize authorized people by how they walk, sold to building-security and logistics customers — a different product and a different market from HVB's law-enforcement face matching [m16.fact.015]. Priya's new role is to architect a new gait model from scratch; it does not involve face recognition or HVB's watch-list matching at all [m16.fact.016].

Priya gave notice on January 12, 2026, and her last day at HVB was January 30, 2026 [m16.fact.017]. She returned her company laptop, phone, and access badge on her last day, and HVB's own IT security team ran a forensic off-boarding review of her devices and accounts [m16.fact.018]. That review found no company source code, no training data, and no customer files on any personal device or personal cloud account of hers — a fact HVB has not disputed [m16.fact.019]. Her scheduled start date at Sightline was February 9, 2026 [m16.fact.020].

## What Priya did on her way out — the seams

Priya insists, truthfully, that she copied no HVB code or data. But the record has three softer edges that a careful lawyer must surface. First, in her last week she emailed a personal folder of her own materials to her private account — mostly resume drafts and conference talks — and one item in that folder was a hand-drawn architecture diagram she had sketched of a matching pipeline; she says it was a generic teaching diagram she made for a mentoring session, but its provenance is arguable [m16.fact.021]. Second, she kept a personal engineering notebook throughout her career, with her own notes on techniques and lessons learned, and she took that notebook with her, believing it recorded her own general skill rather than any company secret [m16.fact.022]. Third, before she left she had an informal conversation with a former HVB colleague, Naomi Vandermeer, about whether Vandermeer might also be a fit at Sightline — a conversation that HVB could try to frame as solicitation [m16.fact.023].

There is also a fact Priya is deeply reluctant to admit: roughly a year before she left, in a frustrated moment after the promotion fell through, she told a coworker over drinks that she "knew HVB's secret sauce cold and could rebuild the whole engine anywhere in a month" [m16.fact.024]. It was bravado, not a plan, but it is exactly the kind of statement HVB would seize on to argue inevitable disclosure, and she knows it.

## HVB's response and the litigation

When HVB learned Priya was going to Sightline, it moved fast. On February 2, 2026, its outside counsel sent a cease-and-desist letter demanding that she not begin work at Sightline and threatening suit [m16.fact.025]. When she did not withdraw, HVB filed an Order to Show Cause seeking a preliminary injunction on February 13, 2026, in the Commercial Division of the Supreme Court of the State of New York, Dutchess County, asking the court to enforce the non-compete, bar her from working at Sightline, and order the return of any confidential material [m16.fact.026]. HVB's central theory is "inevitable disclosure": it argues that because Priya knows the matching engine so intimately, she cannot perform any competing computer-vision job without inevitably using or disclosing its trade secrets [m16.fact.027]. The company frames the engine as its "crown jewel," built over years at great expense [m16.fact.028].

Roland Steinhauer, HVB's co-founder and chief technology officer, drove the decision to sue and is the company's declarant on the motion [m16.fact.029]. As a management-level representative of the represented adverse party, Steinhauer may not be contacted directly by defense counsel about the dispute. Privately, Steinhauer concedes he has no proof Priya took anything and is proceeding on assumption and inference [m16.fact.030]. There is also a motive HVB does not put in its papers: two months before Priya resigned, HVB lost a large county-wide public-safety contract bid to Sightline, and the injunction is partly meant to slow a rival and warn other engineers against leaving [m16.fact.031].

## The witnesses

Three fact witnesses fill in the picture. Dominic Frawley, a senior engineer still at HVB, will say the matching engine's methods are drawn from public research and open libraries, were discussed openly inside the company, and were never locked down the way HVB now claims [m16.fact.032]. Beatriz Salcedo, the Sightline vice-president who hired Priya, will say Priya's role is a clean-room gait project in a different market, that Sightline instructed her to bring no HVB materials, and that Sightline built an ethical wall to keep her away from anything resembling HVB's work [m16.fact.033]. Naomi Vandermeer, a former HVB engineer who left in 2024, will say the covenant is boilerplate that every engineer signs without negotiation, that HVB threatened her with the same non-compete when she left but never sued, and that no single person "owns" the engine [m16.fact.034].

## Where the client stands

Priya is frightened. She has not yet started substantive work at Sightline — her start was paused pending this fight — and she cannot afford to be out of work for two years, or to be branded a thief in an industry where reputation travels fast [m16.fact.035]. She wants three things: to keep the Sightline job, to defeat or narrow the injunction, and to protect her professional name [m16.fact.036]. She genuinely does not know what specific thing HVB will point to as "the trade secret," because in her experience there was never one document or one secret — just a large, collaboratively built system resting on public foundations [m16.fact.037].
