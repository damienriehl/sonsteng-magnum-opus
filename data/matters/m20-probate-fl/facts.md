# Ground-Truth Facts — In re the Estate of Harold W. Prewitt

*Master narrative for matter m20. Every material fact carries a sequential anchor `[m20.fact.NNN]`. Student-facing documents stay facts-only; the jurisdiction is the State of Florida, and the contest is pending in the Probate Division of the Circuit Court, Twelfth Judicial Circuit, in and for Manatee County. This file is the canonical record against which personas, exhibits, and the rubric resolve.*

## The family and the property

Harold W. Prewitt was born in 1948 and worked for decades as a civil engineer before retiring [m20.fact.001]. His first wife, Nadia Prewitt — the mother of his only child — died in 2015 after a long marriage [m20.fact.002]. That child is Dale Prewitt, who is forty-six, lives in Ohio, and works as a regional sales manager [m20.fact.003]. In 2018 Harold married his second wife, Vivian Prewitt-Cole; they were married for roughly seven years until his death [m20.fact.004]. Vivian, who is sixty-three, is a former hospital nurse; in the last years of Harold's life she gave up her own apartment and part-time nursing work to care for him full time [m20.fact.005].

The couple lived at 41 Heron Point Lane, a waterfront house in Manatee County that was Harold's homestead [m20.fact.006]. Harold had bought the house in 2002, during his first marriage, and held title in his sole name [m20.fact.007]. The home is by far the estate's largest asset, worth approximately $720,000; the total estate, including bank and brokerage accounts and personal property, is worth approximately $1,050,000 [m20.fact.008]. Before they married in 2018, Harold and Vivian signed a prenuptial agreement; in it Vivian waived her elective share, and — in language the parties now read very differently — waived rights in Harold's homestead [m20.fact.009].

## The prior estate plan

Harold signed a will in 2019, the year after he remarried [m20.fact.010]. That prior will gave Vivian a life estate in the home — the right to live there for the rest of her life — with the remainder passing to Dale, and it split the residue of the estate between Vivian and Dale [m20.fact.011]. In other words, Harold's earlier plan let Vivian stay in the house but ultimately preserved the family home for his son.

## Harold's decline and Vivian's caregiving

Beginning in 2023, Harold's health declined [m20.fact.012]. His longtime physician, Dr. Elias Vandermolen, diagnosed him with early-stage Parkinson's disease, marked by a hand tremor and some slowing, along with cardiac problems [m20.fact.013]. In 2023 Vivian became his primary caregiver, managing his medications, his appointments, and his daily needs [m20.fact.014]. That same year Harold signed a durable power of attorney naming Vivian as his agent [m20.fact.015], and Vivian was added as a joint signer on Harold's checking account so she could pay his bills [m20.fact.016].

Dale was largely absent during this period. He had not visited his father in about two years before Harold's death [m20.fact.017]. The estrangement traced back to 2016, when Harold paid off roughly $80,000 of Dale's business and personal debts; relations between father and son soured over money afterward [m20.fact.018]. Dale resented that Vivian lived with his father and, in his view, controlled who had access to Harold [m20.fact.019].

## The new will and trust

In early 2024, arrangements were made for Harold to update his estate plan, and he came to the offices of Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP [m20.fact.020]. Vivian drove Harold to the office and waited in the reception area during the meeting [m20.fact.021]. Amara Ellingboe, the senior attorney, met alone with Harold; Vivian was not in the room while the plan was discussed [m20.fact.022]. Harold told Ellingboe that he wanted Vivian to have the home outright — not just a life estate — and to be the main beneficiary of his estate [m20.fact.023]. He gave two reasons: that Vivian had given up a great deal to care for him, and that Dale had already received a large gift during his lifetime and had grown distant [m20.fact.024].

The new will was executed on March 14, 2024, at the firm's office [m20.fact.025]. It was witnessed by two attesting witnesses — Gloria Tancredi, a longtime friend of Harold's, and Corinne Yarbrough, the firm's paralegal — and it was notarized and included a self-proving affidavit [m20.fact.026]. Under the new plan, the will devises the Heron Point home to Vivian and pours the residue of the estate into a revocable living trust that benefits Vivian; Dale receives a cash bequest of $50,000 [m20.fact.027]. Vivian is named personal representative [m20.fact.028]. At Harold's request, the firm also drafted an explanatory letter, written in Harold's voice, setting out in plain terms why he was leaving the home to Vivian and a smaller sum to Dale [m20.fact.029]. Harold signed the explanatory letter the same day he signed the will, and it was kept with the will in the firm's file [m20.fact.030].

## The capacity evidence

Dr. Vandermolen examined Harold on February 28, 2024 — about two weeks before the will signing [m20.fact.031]. In his notes he recorded that Harold understood the general nature and extent of his property, knew the natural objects of his bounty — his wife and his son — and understood that he was making a will [m20.fact.032]. He noted that although Harold had a visible tremor and some lapses concerning recent day-to-day events, his memory of long-standing matters and family relationships was intact [m20.fact.033]. Gloria Tancredi recalls that Harold was tired that afternoon but clearly stated he wanted Vivian to have the house [m20.fact.034]. By Vivian's account, Harold was alert and himself on the morning of the signing [m20.fact.035].

## The homestead problem

Several facts make the home itself a battleground. The Heron Point house was Harold's homestead, and at his death Harold was survived by a spouse, Vivian, and by one adult son, Dale; he had no minor children [m20.fact.036]. The new will devises that homestead outright to Vivian [m20.fact.037]. Dale contends that under Florida's homestead protections the home could not be devised away from him as Harold's descendant, and that the house should pass to him [m20.fact.038]. Dale leans heavily on the prenuptial agreement: he argues that because Vivian waived her rights in Harold's homestead, the devise of the home to her is ineffective, so the property must pass to Harold's descendant — himself [m20.fact.039]. The estate contends that a homestead may be devised to a surviving spouse where the decedent leaves no minor child, and that Vivian's waiver of her own homestead rights does not void an affirmative gift of the home to her under the will [m20.fact.040].

## Death and the contest

Harold died on February 20, 2025, at the Heron Point home — about eleven months after signing the new will [m20.fact.041]. Vivian filed the will for probate in the Probate Division of the Circuit Court in Manatee County [m20.fact.042]. In April 2025 the court entered an order admitting the will to probate and appointing Vivian as personal representative [m20.fact.043]. In June 2025 Dale filed a petition to revoke probate and an objection to the will [m20.fact.044]. Dale alleges that Harold lacked testamentary capacity, that Vivian exercised undue influence over him, and — separately — that the homestead could not be devised as the will directs [m20.fact.045]. He claims that Vivian isolated his father and controlled his finances [m20.fact.046].

## The complicating facts

Several facts cut against the estate's clean story. Vivian was the one who first raised the idea that Harold "should update his will," and Vivian made the appointment with the firm [m20.fact.047]. About two months before the signing, Vivian and Dale had a bitter phone argument in which Dale said the house should come to him and accused Vivian of being after Harold's money [m20.fact.048]. Between 2023 and 2024, Vivian withdrew roughly $12,000 from Harold's account for care expenses and reimbursements to herself, and some of those withdrawals are not well documented [m20.fact.049]. Vivian is worried that the withdrawals could look like self-dealing [m20.fact.050].

Marisol Delacroix, a home health aide, worked weekday afternoons at the Heron Point home through 2023 and into 2025 [m20.fact.051]. She observed that Harold had "good days and bad days," steady and clear at some times and confused or exhausted at others [m20.fact.052]. Delacroix once overheard Vivian tell Harold, in a moment of frustration, that "Dale only calls when he needs money" [m20.fact.053]. Delacroix was paid through Vivian and is uneasy about being pulled into the family dispute, fearing her account will be called biased [m20.fact.054].

Dale's own position is not free of complication. He privately concedes that his father's decision to favor Vivian hurt him and that money is part of why he is contesting [m20.fact.055]. Dale is carrying significant debts, and the Heron Point home is the asset that would make him whole [m20.fact.056].

## The firm's position

Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP drafted the will, the revocable trust, and the explanatory letter, and the firm now defends the will against the contest; because Amara Ellingboe drafted and supervised the execution, she is a potential witness on capacity and the absence of undue influence [m20.fact.057]. The firm handled the original estate-planning work for a flat fee of $2,400, which was paid in 2024 [m20.fact.058].

The defense therefore turns on questions the contest deliberately blurs together. First, whether Harold understood, at the moment he signed, what he owned, who his family was, and what a will does. Second, whether Vivian overbore Harold's free will, given that she lived with him, held his power of attorney, arranged the appointment, and stood to gain the home. Third, a pure question of law layered on top of both: whether Florida's homestead-devise rules permit the gift of the house to Vivian at all, and what the prenuptial waiver does to that analysis. The record supplies evidence on both sides, and the estate must be candid about the facts that cut against it — the undocumented withdrawals, Vivian's role in setting up the meeting, and the overheard remark about Dale — while marshaling the physician's assessment, the private attorney meeting, the disinterested witness, and Harold's own signed letter of explanation.
