# Ground-Truth Facts — Halden Regional Transit District v. Osgard (Arbitration)

*Master narrative for matter m01. This is the closed world of the exercise. Every material fact carries a sequential anchor. Student-facing packets stay facts-only; the law is the student's research problem.*

## The parties and the workplace

Marceline Osgard is a diesel mechanic who worked for the Halden Regional Transit District for eleven years before her discharge [m01.fact.001]. The Halden Regional Transit District ("the District") is a public agency that operates fixed-route buses and paratransit vans in and around Norsholm, in Halden County [m01.fact.002]. Osgard was hired in 2014 and rose to the position of lead mechanic on the District's brake and air-brake systems, a role she held for roughly the last six years of her employment [m01.fact.003]. Her performance reviews through 2023 rated her "meets or exceeds expectations," and she twice received the shop's annual safety-record recognition [m01.fact.004].

Osgard is a member of the Meridian Alliance of Public Workers, Local 7 ("the Union"), which represents the District's maintenance and operations employees [m01.fact.005]. The relationship is governed by a collective-bargaining agreement between the District and the Union [m01.fact.006]. Under the agreement, a covered employee may be disciplined or discharged only for just cause, and any grievance that is not resolved in the internal steps may be submitted to final and binding arbitration before a neutral arbitrator [m01.fact.007].

## The new superintendent and the pull-out pressure

In early 2024, the District hired Gerald Rennick as maintenance superintendent, making him Osgard's second-line supervisor [m01.fact.008]. Rennick came in under a mandate to improve the District's "on-time pull-out" rate — the percentage of buses that leave the garage on schedule for the morning service run [m01.fact.009]. Mechanics understood that Rennick tracked pull-out metrics closely and pressed the shop to return buses to service quickly [m01.fact.010]. Osgard and other mechanics felt the new pressure conflicted with the time a careful brake inspection takes [m01.fact.011].

## The three buses

On the morning of September 22, 2025, Osgard inspected three buses assigned for the next day's pull-out — units 411, 418, and 426 — and found what she recorded as weeping brake air lines and slow air-pressure loss on the rear service brakes [m01.fact.012]. She placed a red "Out of Service" tag on each of the three units and entered the defects in the shop's repair-order system [m01.fact.013]. Rennick reviewed the tags that afternoon and directed Osgard to clear at least two of the three so they would be available for the morning run [m01.fact.014]. Osgard refused, telling Rennick that she would not certify the buses roadworthy until the air-line leaks were repaired [m01.fact.015]. Rennick told her the units "looked fine to him" and that she was creating a service problem [m01.fact.016].

A later inspection by an outside contractor confirmed air-line leaks on units 411 and 426, but found that unit 418 held pressure within tolerance [m01.fact.017]. The parties dispute whether Osgard's original tag on unit 418 was a reasonable precaution or an overreach [m01.fact.018].

## The complaint chain and the oversight board

On October 1, 2025, after the dispute continued for several days, Osgard sent an email describing the brake defects and the return-to-service pressure to the District's internal safety complaint chain, and copied the Regional Transit Safety Oversight Board, an external body [m01.fact.019]. She wrote that she believed she was being pressured to return unsafe vehicles to service [m01.fact.020]. Osgard does not know whether the Oversight Board ever opened an investigation in response to her email [m01.fact.021]. Rennick and the District's operations director viewed the external email as going outside the chain of command [m01.fact.022].

## The discharge

Between October 6 and October 14, 2025, friction between Osgard and Rennick escalated [m01.fact.023]. On October 9, Rennick instructed Osgard to leave the brake bay and report to a different work assignment; Osgard first walked to the parts room to secure her tools and log out of the repair system before complying, which took roughly fifteen minutes [m01.fact.024]. Rennick characterized that delay as Osgard "leaving her assigned post" and refusing a direct instruction [m01.fact.025]. On October 15, 2025, the District discharged Osgard [m01.fact.026]. The written Notice of Discharge cited "repeated insubordination — refusal of direct supervisory instructions" and "leaving an assigned work post," and referenced her prior disciplinary record [m01.fact.027].

## The disciplinary record

Osgard's personnel file contains a 2023 written reminder about timekeeping and two supervisor notes [m01.fact.028]. One note, dated November 2024, describes Osgard as "argumentative and resistant to direction" [m01.fact.029]. Osgard contends the 2024 note appeared only after she first raised concerns about rushed brake work, and that no such note ever appeared in her prior nine years [m01.fact.030]. The District maintains the note reflected a genuine performance concern independent of any safety complaint [m01.fact.031].

## The grievance

The Union filed a grievance on Osgard's behalf on October 20, 2025, asserting that the discharge lacked just cause and was retaliation for protected safety complaints [m01.fact.032]. The grievance moved through the agreement's internal steps and was denied at each step, with the District's step responses emphasizing insubordination rather than the earlier safety dispute [m01.fact.033]. Union steward Loretta Bexley, who processed the grievance, observed that the District's stated rationale shifted between steps [m01.fact.034]. The Union advanced the grievance to arbitration under the agreement, and the parties selected Frances Eklund as the neutral arbitrator [m01.fact.035]. A merits hearing is scheduled but has not yet occurred [m01.fact.036].

## Witnesses and the seams

Theo Dellwood, a fellow mechanic, was working the adjacent bay on September 22 and both saw the red tags and heard Rennick direct Osgard to return the buses to service [m01.fact.037]. Dellwood is willing to describe what he saw but is anxious about his own standing at the District [m01.fact.038]. Dellwood also recalls that unit 418 later passed inspection, which he expects the District to use against Osgard's judgment [m01.fact.039].

Two facts sit closer to the vest. Osgard's insistence on the red tags is driven in part by an incident about two years earlier, when she returned a bus to service under similar pressure and it developed a brake fault on route the same week — a near-miss that badly shook her [m01.fact.040]. Separately, during the October dispute Osgard used her personal phone to record one hallway conversation with Rennick, without telling anyone, and she is uneasy about whether she should have done so [m01.fact.041].

## Money and posture

Osgard is under financial strain since the discharge and has fallen behind on the firm's invoices, which embarrasses her [m01.fact.042]. The matter is being handled by Ellingboe & Ravndal LLP on an hourly basis, with the case in pre-hearing preparation as of June 30, 2026 [m01.fact.043].

## The competing accounts, side by side

The Union's account is that Osgard did exactly what a lead brake mechanic is supposed to do: she found defects, tagged the units, documented them, and declined to certify vehicles she believed were unsafe [m01.fact.044]. On this account the discharge is retaliation dressed up as insubordination, timed to her external safety email and reflected in a disciplinary "record" that materialized only after she began raising concerns [m01.fact.045]. The District's account is that Osgard, whatever her intentions, repeatedly refused direct and lawful instructions from her superintendent, walked away from an assigned post, and had a documented pattern of resisting direction that culminated in an unworkable situation for the shop [m01.fact.046].

Both sides agree on the sequence of events but disagree sharply on characterization and motive [m01.fact.047]. The contractor's mixed inspection result — leaks confirmed on two units, none on the third — gives each side something to argue: the Union points to the two confirmed leaks as vindication of Osgard's core judgment, while the District points to unit 418 to argue she over-tagged and manufactured a service crisis [m01.fact.048]. Loretta Bexley kept contemporaneous notes of the grievance meetings, and those notes are expected to matter at the hearing because they capture how the District described its reasons at each step [m01.fact.049]. The arbitrator will decide whether the District carried its burden of showing just cause for the discharge and, if not, what remedy — reinstatement, back pay, or both — is appropriate [m01.fact.050].

## Osgard's goals

Osgard's stated goal is reinstatement to her mechanic position with back pay and the removal of the discharge from her record; she cares less about the money than about the finding that she was right to refuse [m01.fact.051]. She is worried that a long fight will exhaust her savings and that a settlement offering money but no reinstatement would feel like an admission she was wrong [m01.fact.052].
